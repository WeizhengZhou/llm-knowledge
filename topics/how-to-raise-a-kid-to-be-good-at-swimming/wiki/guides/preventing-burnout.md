# Preventing Burnout

## Overview

According to a PMC review (PMC6805065) citing a 2024 AAP report, 70% of youth athletes drop out of competitive sports by age 13, often due to burnout or injury. Swimming is not exempt. This guide covers why swimmers quit, how to recognize burnout early, and what parents and coaches can do to keep swimming sustainable.

## Why Swimmers Quit

According to a PMC systematic review (PMC5968952), six studies (1982-2008) involving 727 participants aged 5-30 were analyzed. The study population was predominantly from Canada (4 studies), USA (1), and Spain (1), and 65.74% were female.

According to a PMC systematic review (PMC5968952), the most common dropout reason across all age groups and genders was 'had other things to do,' followed by 'lack of enjoyment' and boredom.

According to the same review (PMC5968952), the 15-18 age group valued reasons like 'no team work,' parental/peer pressure, and insufficient challenge more than the 10-14 group; overall dropout concerns peak at ages 13-18.

According to PMC5968952, females more frequently cited 'did not like the pressure' as a dropout reason compared to males.

The PMC systematic review (PMC5968952) concluded that most dropout reasons were controllable by athletes, suggesting intervention potential through environmental modifications and motivation enhancement.

## Burnout Risk Factors

### Early Specialization

Research (PMC6805065) reports that athletes who specialized before age 12 had 3.76 times higher burnout rates than those who specialized after age 15. However, a separate systematic review (PMC5968952) found no evidence that early specialization is a direct predictor of burnout or dropout. The distinction is between an observed association and a causal relationship.

The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends against single-sport specialization until at least age 15-16. Swimming is not listed among the exceptions to this recommendation (see [[early-specialization]]).

### Overtraining

The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) recommends that children should not participate in more hours per week of organized sports than their age in years (e.g., a 10-year-old: max 10 hours/week). This guideline is also endorsed by the NSCA.

Use these LTAD-aligned benchmarks to evaluate whether your child's current training load is appropriate:

| Age Group | Typical Practices/Week | Session Length | Approx. Weekly Hours |
|-----------|----------------------|----------------|---------------------|
| 8 & Under | 2-3 | 60 min | 2-3 hrs |
| 9-10 | 4-5 | 60-75 min | 4-6 hrs |
| 11-12 | 5-6 | 75-90 min | 6-9 hrs |
| 13-14 | 5-6 | 90-120 min | 8-12 hrs |

(Source: SwimSwam general guidance; individual clubs vary. See [[developmental-pathway]] for the full training volume table including high school.)

If your child's club significantly exceeds these benchmarks for their age group, or exceeds the AAP's hours-per-week = age-in-years guideline, ask the coach to explain the rationale. If the coach dismisses the concern, that is a red flag (see [[choosing-a-club-and-coach]]).

One compiled source suggests that if a swimmer's resting heart rate is elevated by 10-15 beats per minute for 2-3 consecutive days, their body may be struggling to meet training demands. If you observe these signs, the first step is reducing training load -- ask the coach for a reduced practice schedule or a rest week. If the coach is resistant to reducing load when a swimmer is showing overtraining signs, that is a red flag for the club's training philosophy. Consult a sports medicine professional if symptoms persist.

### Loss of Intrinsic Motivation

A 2025 MDPI Sports study found that amotivation (lack of motivation) is the strongest predictor of dropout, while swimmers with high intrinsic motivation have lower dropout risk.

Intrinsic motivation is eroded by:
- Excessive parental pressure or directiveness (see [[parent-playbook]])
- Over-emphasis on results vs. process
- Loss of autonomy over training decisions
- Social isolation or poor team environment
- Repetitive, monotonous training without variety

## Warning Signs

**In the child:**
- Dreading practice or finding excuses to skip
- Persistent fatigue or frequent illness
- Declining performance despite maintained effort
- Loss of interest in goals or competition
- Social withdrawal from teammates
- Increased irritability or emotional volatility around swimming

**In the training environment:**
- Training volume exceeding the AAP's age-in-years guideline
- No rest weeks or deload periods built into the season
- Punitive coaching (extra yardage as punishment, public criticism)
- Year-round training with no off-season break

## Prevention Strategies

### For Parents

1. **Protect intrinsic motivation** -- follow the [[parent-playbook]] guidelines on what to say and what not to say
2. **Encourage multi-sport participation** -- see [[swimming-and-other-sports]] and [[early-specialization]]; at least one other sport per year through age 12
3. **Support rest and recovery** -- advocate for at least one full day off per week and periodic breaks
4. **Monitor enjoyment** -- regularly ask "Are you having fun?" and take the answer seriously
5. **Model healthy perspective** -- your attitude sets the tone

### For the Training Environment

- Ensure the [[choosing-a-club-and-coach|coach]] prioritizes technique and fun alongside competition
- Look for programs that build variety into training (cross-training, dryland, team-building activities)
- Confirm the club has an off-season or reduced-volume period

### The Off-Ramp

If a child wants to take a break or quit, support the decision. A swimmer who takes a year off and returns motivated will develop better than one who grinds through years of declining enthusiasm. Swimming World Magazine warns that without LTAD adherence, swimmers often 'plateau and get to the point where they disengage' during adolescence when growth slows.

## Common Mistakes

- **Dismissing burnout as laziness:** If a previously motivated child suddenly resists swimming, investigate rather than push harder.
- **Blaming the child for "lack of commitment":** The environment (coaching, parental pressure, training load) is usually the problem, not the child.
- **Treating the first sign of disengagement as permanent:** A reduced schedule, a brief break, or a change of club can revive interest.
