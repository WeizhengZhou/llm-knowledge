## SECTION 2: Asian Enrollment Trends Beyond Admit Rates

### Yield Differences

No official yield-by-race data is publicly released by any of the schools researched. The following is inferred from enrollment vs. admission statistics where available.

- Harvard Class of 2029 overall yield: 83.6% — among the highest in the country (L1 — Harvard official, via Harvard Magazine)
- MIT does not publicly break out yield by race
- The SFFA-era enrollment shifts reflect a combination of admissions decisions AND yield behavior, but the relative contribution cannot be separated from public data

### Structural Note on Reporting

Multiple institutions changed their racial classification methodology concurrent with the SFFA ruling, adopting the new IPEDS methodology that separately categorizes multiracial students. This makes direct year-over-year comparisons unreliable without controlling for the methodology change. The MIT Class of 2029 article from The Tech (Nov 2025) explicitly flagged this: "the new IPEDS methodology... makes direct comparisons to older demographic reports limited." (L2)

### Black and Hispanic Counterfactual

Across nearly all schools, the post-SFFA classes show meaningful declines in Black and Hispanic enrollment. This is directly relevant to understanding Asian American gains: the "space" created by the SFFA ruling largely shifted toward white and Asian students, with the distribution varying by institution. (L2 — Harvard Magazine, Inside Higher Ed analysis)

## SECTION 3: Race-Blind Policy — Proxies and Strategies

### How Schools Are Adapting

**A. Socioeconomic Proxies**
Universities expanded socioeconomic preferences, awarding admission weight based on factors including below-average parental income, first-generation college status, neighborhood housing turnover, and single-parent household status. Some institutions use big-data reverse engineering: social factors such as "single-female-headed household," "neighborhood housing turnover rate," and "criminal-justice involvement" are statistically strong predictors of race and are being deployed as proxies. (L3 — City Journal analysis; Inside Higher Ed opinion pieces)

Observation: These proxies systematically disadvantage affluent applicants of any race — including affluent Asian American students from Bay Area private schools — because socioeconomic proxies provide no boost to students who are racially Asian but economically privileged.

**B. Geographic Diversity / Rural Recruitment**
Yale, Princeton, and other selective institutions have pivoted to alternative recruitment strategies including QuestBridge programs, rural recruitment initiatives, and use of Harvard's Opportunity Atlas to identify underrepresented geographic communities. (L2 — Harvard Magazine, Sept 2024)

Impact on Bay Area private school students: Geographic diversity proxies provide no benefit to Bay Area applicants, who come from a heavily-represented high-income metro area. This applies regardless of race.

**C. Identity/Diversity Essays**
Among the top 65 colleges examined in one study, 43 had essay prompts addressing diversity, identity, or adversity in the latest application cycle, up from 35 the year before. The Supreme Court's SFFA majority opinion stated schools may still consider how an applicant's personal experiences with race bore on their character, provided essays are evaluated as individual narratives rather than racial identity per se. (L2 — Inside Higher Ed, examining admissions essays post-SFFA, May 2024)

As of late 2025, the Trump administration's Department of Education has written that universities "requiring" students to disclose their race on essays "are likely illegally attempting to do indirectly what cannot be done directly." This creates legal pressure against the essay workaround strategy. (L2 — McGuireWoods alert citing DOE guidance, Feb 2025)

**D. Compliance Questions**
Several analyses note that Harvard, Princeton, Yale, Penn, and Duke achieved near-identical racial composition to pre-SFFA classes, with cumulative Black and Hispanic shares declining by only 0.6 pp on average. This has prompted legal scrutiny about whether these schools are actually complying with the ruling or using stealthy socioeconomic proxies. SFFA sent threatening letters to Duke, Princeton, and Yale in 2024. (L2 — BestColleges; Inside Higher Ed, Sept 2024)

### Specific Impact on Bay Area Private School Applicants

No school-specific demographic data exists in public sources breaking out admissions outcomes by feeder high school and race combined. The following is structural analysis from available evidence:

- Bay Area private school students (Harker, Menlo, SHS, Crystal Springs, etc.) are predominantly affluent and from high-opportunity ZIP codes — they receive no socioeconomic, geographic, or first-gen boost
- These students compete in the most statistically overcrowded applicant pools: high test scores, high GPA, strong extracurriculars
- The SFFA ruling does not address the structural factors that most disadvantage academically strong Asian American applicants from elite private high schools: (1) legacy preferences, (2) athlete recruiting, and (3) donor preferences — all of which disproportionately benefit white applicants at schools that retained these preferences
- Harvard's research (pre-SFFA trial) showed legacies were 5.7x more likely to be admitted than comparable non-legacies; this advantage remains in force at Harvard, Yale, Stanford, and Duke (L2 — Wikipedia/legacy preferences; academic sources)
- Asian American applicants from affluent Bay Area private schools face a compounded disadvantage: they receive no race boost (pre-SFFA), no socioeconomic boost (post-SFFA), and face legacy/ALDC competition from predominantly white families who attended the same elite institutions

### Does Removing Race-Conscious Admissions While Keeping Legacy Help or Hurt Asian Applicants?

Analysis from available evidence:

The SFFA ruling removed race-conscious admissions but did not touch legacy/athlete/donor (ALDC) preferences. Research from the Harvard admissions data (used in the SFFA trial) showed:
- Legacy applicants: admitted at 5.7x the rate of comparable non-legacies
- Athletes: admitted at very high rates, predominantly white in revenue sports
- ALDC applicants: approximately 43% of white admits had ALDC status vs. ~15% of Asian admits

The net result is that post-SFFA, the largest remaining structural preference at Harvard, Yale, Princeton, and Duke continues to advantage white applicants over Asian American applicants. Removing race-conscious admissions narrowed one structural barrier for Asian applicants while leaving the other intact. The enrollment data (modest Asian gains) is consistent with this interpretation: benefits of removing the race-conscious ceiling were partially offset by the continued legacy/ALDC floor for white admits. (L2 — SFFA v. Harvard trial record; academic analysis cited in search results)

For Bay Area private school Asian American applicants specifically: the California legacy ban at Stanford is the single largest structural change post-SFFA that benefits them directly. At Harvard, Yale, Princeton, and Duke, legacy preferences persist and the family planning calculus remains unchanged.

## SECTION 5: Long-Term Projections (2035–2040 Horizon)

### Expert Predictions Available (Through Research Date)

**Prediction accuracy check:** Pre-SFFA researchers had forecast "less than two percentage points" average gains for Asian Americans nationally if affirmative action were eliminated. The Class of 2028 data — which showed Harvard flat, Stanford +1 pp, Cornell +2 pp, Duke -6 pp, Yale -6 pp — is broadly consistent with this prediction at the aggregate national level, even if individual schools diverged significantly. (L2 — Inside Higher Ed opinion, Feb 2025; NBC News)

**What experts have said about trajectory:**
- Universities with large STEM applicant pools (MIT, Caltech, Harvey Mudd) have seen larger Asian American enrollment increases and appear more likely to stabilize at higher Asian percentages post-SFFA
- Universities with strong legacy/ALDC programs (Harvard, Yale, Princeton) appear to have structural ceilings that limit Asian American gains even post-SFFA
- The new IPEDS reporting methodology introduced alongside SFFA compliance changes means multi-year trend data will not be clean until at least the Class of 2030 (2025 entering cohort), making projections unreliable until then
- The legal environment remains unsettled: SFFA is actively suing or threatening multiple schools; the Trump DOE is restricting identity essays; California has banned legacy admissions; Congressional action on legacy preferences was proposed but not enacted

**No credible expert projections specifically for 2035–2040 were found in available public sources.** The field is too unsettled for such projections to exist with meaningful confidence. (Note: any specific 2035-2040 projections encountered should be treated as L4 speculation unless published in peer-reviewed form.)

### Structural factors likely to persist through 2038 (child's application year):

1. **STEM-heavy schools will likely continue higher Asian American enrollment** — MIT's 38-47% range post-SFFA reflects the underlying applicant pool composition at STEM-focused institutions. This is unlikely to reverse without explicit policy intervention.

2. **Holistic admissions schools (Harvard, Yale, Princeton, Duke) will face continued legal and regulatory pressure** — SFFA is actively monitoring; the Trump DOE is hostile to proxy strategies; additional state-level legacy bans are possible. The trajectory is toward more, not fewer, constraints on these schools' flexibility.

3. **California legacy ban will reshape Stanford admissions from 2026 entering class onward** — this is a concrete structural change for Bay Area families that will have fully taken effect by 2038.

4. **Socioeconomic stratification risk** — if elite schools increase weight on first-gen, Pell-eligible, and geographic diversity factors, affluent Bay Area private school students (of any race) face increased competitive pressure from a growing pool of high-achieving low-income students, including Asian Americans from less affluent backgrounds.

5. **Growing applicant pool from Asian countries** — international Asian students and Asian Americans are both large and growing segments. Whether elite schools impose implicit or explicit caps (as the SFFA case alleged) remains a legal and empirical question.

## SECTION 9: Methodological Notes for Consumer of This Research

1. **Enrollment vs. admission rates:** The data above is almost entirely enrollment data (who matriculated), not admission rate data (who was offered admission). Admission rates by race are not publicly released by any of these schools. Enrollment captures the combined effect of admissions policy AND student yield choices.

2. **Self-identification methodology change:** Multiple schools changed how they count racial identity concurrent with SFFA implementation, adopting IPEDS standards that separately count multiracial students. This creates breaks in the time series. Claims that "Asian American enrollment increased/decreased X points" must be read with this caveat.

3. **Two-year sample is insufficient:** Statistical noise in any given year's applicant pool can produce swings of several percentage points. The MIT data (47% in Class of 2028, 38% in Class of 2029) illustrates this. Drawing strategy conclusions from one or two data points is unreliable.

4. **California law changes the Stanford calculus:** The legacy ban (AB 1780, effective Sept 2025) is a meaningful structural change for Stanford specifically. Stanford was the most important school for Bay Area families where legacy preferences existed; that preferences ended effective for the Class of 2030 (entering fall 2026) and beyond.

5. **The 2038 applicant will apply under rules not yet known:** The legal landscape around SFFA compliance, identity essays, and proxy strategies is actively contested. Multiple court cases are in progress. The Trump administration has added new dimensions (hostility to diversity essays, potential pressure on international student enrollment). Any strategy built around today's rules needs flexibility.
