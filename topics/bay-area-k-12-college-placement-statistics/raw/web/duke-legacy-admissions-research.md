## Q1: Duke Legacy Admit Rate vs. Overall Admit Rate

### Overall Admit Rates (most recent confirmed data)

**Class of 2029 (2024-25 cycle):**
- Total applications: 58,712 (record high)
- Overall accept rate: ~5.2% (CDS figure) / ~5.7% (some sources)
- ED accept rate (Class of 2029): 12.8% (record low at time) — Source: Duke Chronicle (S2 reporting)
- RD accept rate (Class of 2029): ~3.67%

**Class of 2030 (2025-26 cycle, ED only released as of Dec 2025):**
- ED applications received: 6,159 (7% drop from prior year)
- ED admits: 847 (731 ED + 116 QuestBridge)
- ED accept rate: 13.8% (slightly up from 12.8% and 12.9% prior two years) — Source: S3

### Legacy Admit Rate Estimates

CONFIDENCE NOTE: Duke does not publicly release a specific legacy acceptance rate. All figures below are estimates or extrapolations.

**Claim: Legacy applicants at 12 elite colleges (including Duke) had a 37% acceptance rate vs. 9.5% for non-legacy applicants at the same schools.**
- Source: NYT/Opportunity Insights study (July 2023), cited verbatim in Duke Chronicle opinion piece (S1, Feb 2025)
- Note: This is a pooled figure across 12 elite colleges; Duke-specific legacy rate is not separately published.
- Confidence: L2 (single authoritative source — academic study reported by NYT, cited by Duke's student newspaper)

**Claim: Legacy applicants at Duke historically enjoy acceptance rates estimated at roughly 17%–25%.**
- Source: Admissions consulting aggregators (AdmitStudio, AdmissionSight) — L3
- Basis: Industry estimates; not from Duke-published data
- Confidence: L3

**Claim: 17% of Class of 2027 survey respondents identified as legacy students.**
- Source: Duke Chronicle survey of Class of 2027 — L2
- Confidence: L2 (survey-based, not admissions office data; subject to self-selection bias)

**Claim: Legacy applicants automatically receive a second review of their application; non-legacy applications may receive only one review.**
- Source: Koppelman Group (S4), citing admissions consulting knowledge
- Confidence: L3

**Claim: Legacy applicants in the top 0.1% income bracket are seven times as likely to be admitted as applicants with the same test scores.**
- Source: NYT/Opportunity Insights study, cited in S1
- Confidence: L2

**Bottom line for positioning:** No single verified Duke-specific legacy rate exists in public record. The multi-school 37% vs 9.5% figure (L2) is the most credible benchmark. The 17–25% range (L3) is an industry estimate.

## Q2: Duke Legacy Policy Changes Post-SFFA (after June 2023)

**Claim: Duke has NOT eliminated or formally modified its legacy preference policy after the June 2023 SFFA ruling.**
- Source: SFFA's September 2024 letter to Duke, reported by Duke Chronicle and BestColleges (S9)
- SFFA's letter alleged Duke "refused to eliminate legacy preferences" and stated Asian American enrollment drops were inconsistent with race-neutral admissions
- Duke did not publicly rebut the legacy-preservation allegation
- Confidence: L2

**Claim: Only "a handful" of selective colleges have dropped legacy since SFFA (June 2023). Duke is not among them.**
- Schools that dropped legacy post-SFFA include: Johns Hopkins (2022, pre-SFFA), MIT (never used it formally), Amherst (2022). Duke is not in this group.
- Source: Multiple aggregated news reports
- Confidence: L2

**Claim: Duke's Pell Grant-eligible first-year enrollment doubled from 11% (Class of 2026) to 22% (Class of 2028 and Class of 2029).**
- Source: Duke Chronicle (S2), confirmed in Class of 2029 profile data
- This was cited by SFFA as evidence Duke was trying to appear to compensate for not dropping legacy while remaining suspicious about Asian enrollment
- Confidence: L2

**Claim: SFFA threatened litigation against Duke (September 2024) alleging continued race-consciousness in admissions, using the Asian enrollment drop as evidence.**
- Source: Duke Chronicle (S2), BestColleges (S9)
- Duke did not publicly acknowledge changing any policy in response
- Confidence: L2

**Claim: Duke and 31 other colleges were accused (August 2025) of violating federal antitrust laws through Early Decision programs, which plaintiffs argue constitute price-fixing.**
- Source: Duke Chronicle (S3, Dec 2025)
- This is separate from legacy; Duke did not comment
- Confidence: L2

## Q3: Graduate Degree Legacy — MS vs. Undergraduate Legacy

This is the most critical distinction for this positioning case (both parents hold Duke MS degrees, not Duke undergraduate degrees).

### What Duke's Official Pages Say

**Claim: Duke's Alumni Admissions page describes its services as helping alumni "whose children or grandchildren are interested in applying for undergraduate admission to Duke" — but does not explicitly define whether graduate or only undergraduate degrees qualify as legacy.**
- Source: alumni.duke.edu/benefits-services/alumni-admissions (S5, L1-official)
- The page's language is ambiguous: it describes the target audience for alumni services, not the admissions policy definition
- Confidence: L1 for the language used; L5 for any inference about what it means for grad degrees

**Claim: Duke defines legacy as "children and grandchildren of alumni" without specifying degree type in its public-facing materials.**
- Source: Koppelman Group (S4), CollegeVine (L3)
- No Duke official source was found that explicitly states "graduate degrees do not count" or "graduate degrees count equally"
- Confidence: L3 (aggregator interpretation of Duke's unstated policy)

### What Peer School Patterns Suggest (context only)

From College Confidential forum discussion (S10, L4):
- Yale and Stanford explicitly include graduate students in legacy consideration
- Harvard and Dartmouth specify undergraduate-only
- Duke's position was not specified by any forum participant with firsthand knowledge
- Source: L4 (community forum, not verified)

### Expert Interpretation (L3/L4 signals)

Multiple admissions consulting sources indicate:
- "Elite schools will give more weight to a parent who attended undergraduate, rather than a graduate program" — PrepScholar (L3)
- "Undergraduate admissions offices will be most interested if your parents went there for their undergraduate degrees. If your parents instead got professional degrees and not their undergraduate degrees from the school, it's less likely you'd be counted as a legacy" — DC College Counseling (L3)
- One forum participant noted grad school connections "probably won't help much" unless the alum is a significant donor — L4

### Critical Gap

No L1 or L2 source explicitly states Duke's policy on graduate-only legacy. This is an unresolved question requiring direct contact with Duke's Office of Undergraduate Admissions.

**For positioning purposes:** Treat graduate-only legacy as materially weaker than undergraduate legacy. The advantage, if any, is likely modest and not equivalent to full legacy status. Do not build a strategy on legacy as a primary differentiator unless one parent holds a Duke undergraduate degree.

## Q4: Double Legacy — Both Parents as Duke Alumni

**Claim: Having both parents as alumni ("double legacy") confers additional advantage beyond single legacy at most selective schools.**
- Source: Research cited by admissions consulting sources (S4); 2011 study of 30 elite colleges found "primary legacies" (one or both parents as undergrad alumni) had a 45.1% increase in acceptance chances on average.
- This study predates SFFA but is the most cited quantitative reference.
- Confidence: L3 (aggregator citing older academic study, not Duke-specific)

**Claim: Duke has no publicly documented "double legacy" policy distinct from single legacy.**
- No L1 or L2 source distinguishes double from single legacy in Duke's process
- The procedural advantage described (second review of application) appears to apply to any legacy, not scaled by number of alumni parents
- Confidence: L2 (absence of evidence — duke publishes nothing on this distinction)

**For positioning purposes:** Double alumni status (even MS-only) likely signals stronger affiliation than single-parent alumni status, but without undergraduate legacy as the foundation, the incremental value of "both parents attended Duke" remains speculative.

- Source: Duke Chronicle (S3, Dec 2025) — L2
- Confidence: L2 for ED rates; L2 for total application volumes

**Claim: ED provides a substantial multiplier effect — ~2.5x the overall rate (13.8% vs ~5.2%).**
- This is arithmetic from the data above, not a stated claim.

**Claim: ED typically fills approximately 50% of Duke's incoming class.**
- Source: Admissions consulting aggregators (S4)

- Confidence: L2 (Chronicle language confirms the ~50% proportion)

### Legacy x ED Interaction

**Claim: Legacy advantage at Duke is most concentrated in the ED pool. Legacy applicants are dramatically more likely to apply ED.**
- "Legacy students were more likely to apply Early Decision than Regular Decision to Duke — 81.7% versus 54.4%, respectively" (Class of 2025 data)
- Source: Koppelman Group (S4) citing this figure — L3
- Confidence: L3

**Claim: "If a legacy student does not apply early, they give away any advantage they may have had."**
- Source: Koppelman Group (S4) — direct quote — L3
- This reflects the operational consensus among admissions consultants
- Confidence: L3

**Claim: Legacy consideration is "generally more significant when applying Early Decision at Duke."**
- Source: CollegeVine FAQ on Duke legacy (L3)
- Consistent with the 81.7% vs 54.4% ED application rate differential
- Confidence: L3

**Strategic synthesis:** For a legacy applicant (even with graduate-degree-only status), applying ED is mandatory to preserve any possible legacy benefit. RD legacy advantage appears to be minimal to nonexistent.

## Q6: Asian Applicants at Duke — Post-SFFA Data

### Class of 2027 to Class of 2029: Trend Data

**Note on methodology change:** For the Class of 2029, Duke switched to IPEDS standards (single race/ethnicity category per student, totaling to 100%). Prior years allowed multi-category enrollment, so the numbers are NOT directly comparable to earlier classes without adjusting for this.

**Class of 2029 demographic profile (official, December 2025):**
- Asian, Asian-American, or Pacific Islander: 25%
- White: 33%
- Black or African American: 7%
- Hispanic or Latino: 11%
- Native American/Alaska Native/Native Hawaiian: <1%
- Did not report race/ethnicity: 7%
- Foreign citizens (not included in above race categories per IPEDS): 11%
- Source: Duke Chronicle (S2) citing Duke's official Class of 2029 profile — L2/L1

**Under old (pre-IPEDS) methodology:**
- Class of 2028: Asian 29% (first class after SFFA ruling)
- Class of 2027: Asian 35%
- The 6-percentage-point drop (35% to 29%) from Class of 2027 to Class of 2028 triggered SFFA's September 2024 letter threatening litigation.
- Source: Duke Chronicle (S2) — L2
- Confidence: L2

**On cross-class comparability:**
- Duke explicitly stated the Class of 2029 figures "are not comparable to previous years" due to the methodology change.
- The decline from 29% (Class of 2028, old method) to 25% (Class of 2029, new IPEDS method) cannot be interpreted as a continued decline without accounting for methodology.
- Source: Duke Chronicle (S2) — L2
- Confidence: L2

**Claim: SFFA alleged Duke's Asian enrollment drop was "impossible" under race-neutral admissions and threatened litigation in September 2024.**
- Source: Duke Chronicle (S2), SFFA letter quoted
- Duke did not eliminate legacy preferences in response and did not publicly rebut SFFA's methodology argument
- Confidence: L2

**What is NOT available in public record:**
- Duke's Asian-specific accept rate (as opposed to enrollment percentage)
- Any breakdown of Asian admits vs. Asian applicants from any public source
- Post-SFFA Duke admissions office statement specifically addressing Asian admit rates

### Mathematics Program Strength

**Putnam Competition Track Record (Duke math department):**
- Top 10 nationally 22+ times since 1990
- 1st place finishes: 1993, 2000
- 2nd place: 1990, 1997
- 3rd place: 1999, 2001, 2002, 2003, 2004, 2005
- Recent results: #3 (2023), #8 (2021), #12 (2022), #14 (2024)
- Notable Putnam Fellows produced: Melanie Wood (2002, top 5), Kevin Lacker (1998), Nathan Curtis (1998)
- Morgan Prize (top undergraduate mathematics research prize): Melanie Wood (2004, First Prize)
- Source: math.duke.edu/math-competition-honors (S8, L1-official)

**Duke Math Meet (DMM):** Annual regional math competition for high school students hosted at Duke.
- Source: S8 (L1-official)

**Advanced Problem Solving Seminar (Math 283S):** Course designed for students with AIME-level experience; students required to take Putnam exam.
- Source: Duke math department (L1-official)
- This course exists and indicates Duke actively recruits and supports mathematically competitive undergraduates.

**Physics Rankings (context):**
- Duke ranked 40th for Physics in the United States (EduRank)
- Source: edurank.org — L3
- Confidence: L3

**Mathematics Rankings:**
- Duke ranked 29th for Mathematics in the United States, 39th globally (EduRank)
- Source: edurank.org — L3
- Confidence: L3

### Duke vs. MIT/Caltech/Stanford for Competition Math Applicants

No L1 or L2 source directly compares Duke's admit rate or outcomes for math-competition-strong applicants vs. MIT, Caltech, or Stanford. The following is inference from available data:

**MIT:** Overall accept rate ~3.5%. MIT's culture is explicitly STEM-centric and its Putnam team is historically top-ranked. Math olympiad credentials are directly valued. MIT does not use legacy preferences.

**Caltech:** Overall accept rate ~3.78%. Caltech explicitly cites AMC, IMO, and Physics Olympiad in its admissions context. Pure science/math profile maps most directly.

**Stanford:** Overall accept rate ~3.6%. Strong across all STEM fields; holistic review means math competition credentials help but are not decisive alone.

**Duke (inference only):**
- Duke is competitive for STEM applicants but is a "broader" university than Caltech or MIT; holistic profile matters more
- Duke's Pratt School (engineering) is the most STEM-concentrated entry point; competition math credentials help most for applicants to Pratt or the mathematics major track
- Duke's Putnam track record signals it actively seeks mathematically talented students
- Confidence on comparative claims: L3 (inference from rankings and culture, no direct comparative data source)

**For positioning:** A math-competition-strong applicant (AIME/USAMO range) applying to Duke's mathematics or engineering program is a genuine fit. Duke is a stronger target for this profile than peer "big universities" like Penn or Northwestern, but remains weaker on pure math/physics culture than MIT or Caltech. The legacy factor (if operative) would provide additional lift in the ED pool.

## Data Gaps and Cautions

1. **Duke's graduate-vs-undergraduate legacy policy is not publicly defined.** This is the single most important unknown for this case. No L1 or L2 source resolves it. Must either accept ambiguity or contact Duke admissions directly.

2. **No Duke-specific legacy accept rate is publicly reported.** The 37% figure (S1) is across 12 elite colleges; the 17-25% figures are consulting firm estimates.

3. **Class of 2029 vs. 2028 Asian enrollment comparison is complicated by methodology change.** Cannot straightforwardly call the 29% → 25% shift a "decline" without IPEDS-adjusted prior-year data.

4. **The Duke CDS 2023-24 PDF** (https://provost.duke.edu/sites/default/files/CDS-2023-24-FINAL.pdf) is publicly available but was not fully rendered in this session. It would be the most authoritative source for CDS-standard admissions factors and demographic data.
