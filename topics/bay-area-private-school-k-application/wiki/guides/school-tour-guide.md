# School Tour and Open House Guide

## Overview

Open houses and campus tours are the primary way parents evaluate schools before applying. This guide covers what to observe, what to ask, and how to extract information that is not available on the school's website. The source material is primarily from Cardinal Education (L3 admissions consulting firm); actual event structures vary by school.

## What Open Houses Typically Include

Cardinal Education describes typical private school open house components as including welcome sessions with administration, campus tours, classroom observations, Q&A panels, extracurricular showcases, and sometimes individual admissions meetings. Actual event structures vary by school.

Not all schools run the same format. Some schools require tours as a prerequisite to application (e.g., According to Convent & Stuart Hall, campus tours are required and available Monday/Tuesday mornings, 8:45-11:00 a.m.). Others offer optional open houses. Check each school's requirements on their admissions page or [[ravenna-hub|Ravenna Hub]].

## What to Observe Beyond the Brochure

### Student and Teacher Dynamics

Cardinal Education recommends observing beyond the brochure at open houses: student engagement and happiness levels, discussion-based vs. rote classes, student-teacher warmth, hallway behavior, and what is displayed on walls.

These are signals you cannot get from a website. Look for:
- **Are students engaged or compliant?** Engaged students ask questions and make choices. Compliant students follow instructions quietly.
- **How do teachers speak to children?** Warmth, respect, and patience are observable in real-time.
- **What is on the walls?** Student work displayed at eye-level suggests the school values the process, not just polished outcomes.
- **What happens in the hallways?** Transitions between classes reveal how the school manages behavior and community norms.

### K-Specific Observations

Cardinal Education recommends K-specific open house observations: how kindergarteners interact, whether classroom materials are play-based or academic, how teachers speak about development, and what a typical kindergarten day looks like.

For K applicants, pay special attention to:
- **Play vs. academics:** Is the K classroom set up for structured lessons or exploratory play? This reveals the school's philosophy in practice, not just in marketing (see [[pedagogy-philosophy]]).
- **Outdoor space:** K-age children need movement. How much outdoor time is built into the day?
- **Separation handling:** How does the school manage children who struggle to separate from parents? This is a daily reality in kindergarten.

## Five Categories of Questions to Ask

The following question categories are from Cardinal Education (L3 source). Adapt them based on what matters most to your family.

### 1. Questions for Admissions Officers

Cardinal Education recommends asking admissions officers at open houses about what distinguishes the school from competitors, what qualities matter most in applications, the acceptance rate, financial aid options, and how the school handles learning differences.

The acceptance rate question is worth asking even though most schools will not give a precise answer. Their response -- whether they deflect, give a range, or say "we don't publish that" -- is itself informative.

### 2. Questions for Faculty

Cardinal Education recommends asking faculty at open houses about student-to-teacher ratio, academic support for struggling students, parent communication methods, and how teachers differentiate instruction.

The differentiation question is especially important for K: in a class of 20 children with varying readiness levels, how does the teacher serve both the child who is already reading and the child who is still learning letter sounds?

### 3. Questions for Current Parents

Cardinal Education recommends asking current parents at open houses about how parent volunteers are utilized, annual fund expectations, how the school handles conflicts, and how diverse the parent community is.

Current parents are often the most candid source at an open house. Ask about:
- **Annual fund expectations:** This is a real financial consideration beyond tuition (see [[guides/financial-realities]]).
- **Time commitment:** How many hours per week/month does the school expect from parent volunteers?
- **Conflict resolution:** How the school handled a recent disagreement reveals more than any mission statement.

### 4. K-Specific Questions

Based on the Cardinal Education framework, questions specific to kindergarten applicants:
- What does a typical K day look like from arrival to pickup?
- How long is the adjustment period for new kindergarteners?
- What is the homework expectation in K? (This varies enormously by school philosophy.)
- How does the school communicate with K parents about daily progress?

## Getting the Most from Your Visit

### Before the Visit
- Review the school's website thoroughly so you do not waste time on information already available online
- Prepare 3-5 targeted questions that only an in-person visit can answer
- If both parents can attend, divide and observe different areas

Working parents managing the event load across 5-7 schools, including which events are required vs. optional, see [[guides/admissions-strategy-advanced]].

### During the Visit
- Take brief notes immediately after (not during -- you want to be present and engaged)
- Observe the physical space: cleanliness, natural light, classroom size, outdoor areas
- Watch how the admissions team interacts with children who are present

## Using Your Tour Notes to Decide

After visiting 3 or more schools, your notes become a decision tool. Here is how to use them:

**Rate each school on your family's top priorities.** Before touring, identify 3-5 dimensions that matter most (e.g., teacher warmth, play vs. academics balance, outdoor space, diversity, commute). After each visit, rate the school 1-5 on each dimension. A forced ranking after 3+ visits often reveals preferences you did not know you had.

**Distinguish dealbreakers from yellow flags.** A teacher you observed speaking dismissively to a child is a dealbreaker -- it reflects culture, not a bad day. A classroom that felt crowded is a yellow flag worth asking about in follow-up (enrollment may fluctuate). Record these distinctions in your notes while they are fresh.

**Look for patterns across visits.** If every progressive school you visited felt right but the one structured school felt wrong, that is a signal about your family's philosophy preference, not about that one school. If one school's teachers consistently addressed children by name and another's did not, note the pattern.

**Feed observations into your essay.** Specific observations from tours ("we noticed the K teacher called three children by name when they arrived") are the raw material for the "school fit" paragraph in your parent essay. See [[guides/parent-essay-guide]] for how to convert tour notes into essay content.

## Common Mistakes

- **Attending passively:** An open house where you only listen to the presentation and leave gives you nothing the website could not. Engage, ask, observe.
- **Visiting only your top choice:** Tour 3-4 schools minimum. Comparison reveals what matters to you. A school you thought was a backup may surprise you.
- **Bringing your child to adult events:** Unless specifically invited, leave children at home for open houses. You need to focus, and a restless 4-year-old in a presentation is memorable for the wrong reasons.
- **Over-indexing on facilities:** New buildings and high-tech classrooms are nice but do not predict educational quality. Teacher warmth, student engagement, and curricular philosophy matter more.
