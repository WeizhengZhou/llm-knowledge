# Chapter 10: Decisions and Waitlists — A Plan for Every Outcome

---

At noon on March 19, everyone opens the same email at the same time.

That is not a figure of speech. Under the ISSFBA coordinated system, member schools release decisions simultaneously — electronically, via email and the Ravenna platform — between noon and 2:00 p.m. Parents across the Bay Area who have spent the better part of a year on this process are all reading the same kind of message in the same two-hour window. The collective exhale is real. So is the moment of uncertainty that follows, no matter what the email says.

What separates the families who move through that moment with relative steadiness from those who spin out is not the outcome. It is preparation. Not in the wishful sense — not "visualize success" — but in the practical sense: they already know what they're going to do in each scenario before the email arrives. They read the outcome. They turn to the plan they already have.

This chapter gives you that plan.

---

## The Decision-Day Mechanics

Before anything else, know the mechanics of how decisions are released, so you're not surprised by timing or logistics.

**ISSFBA schools (the majority of Bay Area private schools):** Decisions release March 19, noon to 2:00 p.m., via email and Ravenna. Most families see both arrive within minutes of each other.

**A few schools outside this window:**
- Live Oak School: March 17, 4:00 p.m.
- BASIS Independent Silicon Valley: March 18
- Nueva School and Harker School: March 20
- Challenger School, Stratford School: rolling decisions — you may have received these already

**Response deadlines — do not assume March 26:**
- Most ISSFBA schools: March 26
- SF Day School and Live Oak School: March 24
- SF Friends School: March 25

The March 26 date is widely cited, and it is correct for most schools. But "most" is not "all." Read every offer letter on March 19, note its specific deadline, and add it to your calendar before you do anything else. Missing a 48-hour-earlier deadline is a recoverable problem that becomes unrecoverable if you discover it on March 27.

## Scenario 1: You Have One Offer

You opened the email, you're admitted, and this is your one offer. Here is what to do.

**Read the enrollment contract before you sign it.** The contract specifies the enrollment deposit (typically in the range of $1,000–$5,000, though this varies by school and cycle — verify the exact amount in your offer letter), the withdrawal penalty structure, and the start date. Enrollment contracts are binding documents. Read yours.

**Decline other schools promptly.** Once you know your decision, decline every other school by email. You do not owe a detailed explanation. A short, warm note is enough: "We're grateful for the offer and have accepted another school. Thank you for the consideration you gave our family." Send this as soon as you know — within a day or two of accepting your enrollment. Admissions staff will use your decline to move their waitlists.

Why does this matter beyond courtesy? Admissions directors talk to each other. Preschool directors talk to admissions directors. If you ever want to reapply — to this school, to others — how you handled this moment will be remembered.

**If you're also on a waitlist at another school:** You face a real decision, not a hypothetical one. You can accept this enrollment, pay the deposit, and remain on a waitlist. If the waitlisted school offers you a spot, you can accept it and forfeit the deposit — this is ethically accepted practice and is expected within the system. What is not acceptable is stringing a school along on the enrollment side while you quietly wait on another.

Be honest with yourself: is the waitlisted school so meaningfully different for your child that forfeiting the deposit is worth it? That's a question worth answering now, before you're under pressure.

## Scenario 2: You Have Multiple Offers

Multiple offers create their own kind of stress, which is worth naming honestly: the fear of choosing wrong. The wish that the decision could be made for you. The awareness that one of these schools is about to get your child and the other isn't.

Use the full seven days. You are not obligated to decide on March 19. The deadline exists precisely to give you time. Families who rush a multi-offer decision in the first 24 hours often do so out of relief that the process is ending — which is not the same as confidence in the choice.

**What to do with those seven days:**

Revisit each school. If you can, schedule a brief visit or call with admissions before the deadline. Not to negotiate — the offer is the offer — but to ask the one or two questions that genuinely remain. Current families are often willing to take a quick call. Schools are used to these conversations in decision week; it is not unusual or inappropriate to reach out.

Return to your original evaluation criteria. What mattered to you in September — pedagogical approach, community, location, financial reality, your child's particular temperament — matters now. Admissions advisors who work across multiple cycles consistently find that families who return to their original criteria report more confidence in their choice than families who try to construct new criteria under deadline pressure. Your September instincts are data, not noise.

Compare financial aid awards. Aid packages may differ between schools. Calculate the net cost of each — tuition minus aid, including any other fees — and factor that into your decision alongside fit. The chapter on financial realities (Chapter 8) covers how to read aid letters.

**Declining a school you don't choose:** Brief, warm, no elaboration. "Thank you for the offer. We have decided to enroll at another school and appreciate the time you spent with our family." You do not need to explain which school. You do not need to explain why.

Send your declines the same day you accept. Schools are waiting for them.

## Scenario 3: You're Waitlisted at One or More Schools

> **What this means:** You applied. The school found your child and family interesting enough to keep in consideration — but the class filled before a spot opened for you. You are not rejected. You are in a queue.
>
> **How common is it:** Being waitlisted is a normal outcome, not an edge case. Many Bay Area private K applicants are waitlisted at one or more schools each cycle, including families with strong applications and strong fits.
>
> **What you can do now:** The steps below. They are concrete and they matter.
>
> **What not to over-interpret:** A waitlist placement is not a ranked verdict on your child's value or your family's application quality. Schools fill classes that reflect compositional factors — gender balance, neighborhood, preschool backgrounds, sibling spots — that have nothing to do with the quality of individual applicants.

### Within 48 hours of March 19

Contact the admissions office of every school where you're waitlisted. Brief, direct, warm: "We received the waitlist notification and want to confirm that [child's name] remains our first choice. We are actively interested and would be grateful to be considered if a spot becomes available." This is not begging. It is logistics — admissions staff are managing waitlists actively in this window, and families who do not confirm interest may be removed.

If your family is also admitted to another school, you can honestly hold both: accept the enrollment offer at the admitted school (paying the deposit) and remain on the waitlist at the preferred school. This is standard practice. You are not doing anything wrong.

### When to follow up — and how often

Advisors who work across Bay Area admissions cycles recommend one follow-up contact in late March (after the March 26 deadline), then approximately every three to four weeks through May, then taper off. More frequent contact does not accelerate a decision — and some admissions staff have said explicitly that one thoughtful, sincere communication is more effective than a pattern of repeated check-ins.

Each contact can be brief: an email to the admissions office restating interest and providing a relevant update if there is one. If there is no update, say so: "We have no news to share — we just wanted to reaffirm that our family remains actively interested."

Do not call repeatedly. Do not ask the director of admissions to "just let you know where you stand." If the school can tell you whether the waitlist is ranked or pooled, ask once — it's useful information. Most schools won't share it.

### How waitlists move

Advisors who observe Bay Area admissions cycles across multiple years report that waitlist movement tends to happen in waves:

- **Late March — first wave:** The week after the March 26 response deadline, as families with multiple offers choose one school and release others, is when most movement happens. Families waitlisted at their first-choice school while holding an enrollment elsewhere may receive offers in this window.
- **April through May — second wave:** As public school enrollment deadlines approach, some families reassess. More spots open.
- **June through August — trickle:** Families who have been enrolled since March can still change plans. Summer movement is less predictable but real.

This is advisor-observed pattern across cycles, not school-published data. Individual schools do not disclose waitlist movement rates.

If an offer arrives, the school may give you 24 to 48 hours to decide. If you are already enrolled elsewhere, you can accept the new offer and forfeit the prior enrollment deposit — this is expected, not a breach. Communicate promptly either way. A response of "we need 24 hours to discuss" is entirely appropriate.

### Managing public school enrollment while you wait

Public school enrollment timelines in the Bay Area run roughly parallel to and through the private school waitlist period. Most Bay Area districts have kindergarten enrollment deadlines in the February-to-April window, with placement notifications typically in April or May.

Enroll in your public school district as your backup. This does not cost you the waitlist. It gives you a confirmed school for your child regardless of what happens. If a private school waitlist offer arrives after you've enrolled in public school, you can still accept it — public school enrollment is not a binding commitment in the same way a private school deposit is.

Families who skip public school enrollment "to stay focused on the waitlist" sometimes find themselves in July with no school confirmed for their child. Do not put yourself in that position.

### Gideon Hausner Jewish Day School — Round 2

If March results are disappointing, Gideon Hausner runs a formal second admissions round: apply by April 16, decisions announced April 27. This is not a consolation prize — it is a real admissions round with its own cohort. If Hausner's educational approach and community align with your family, the second round is worth considering seriously.

### Challenger and Stratford

Both schools accept applications year-round. You can apply in April or May, after March results, and potentially have a decision before summer. Admissions advisors who work with Bay Area families consistently flag these as options to know about from the beginning of the cycle, not only as late-stage pivots — but they are equally available to you now.

## Scenario 4: No Offers

If you received no offers, the first thing to do is breathe. Then read this section in full before you do anything else.

> **What this means:** You are in a competitive pool where class composition, sibling seats, financial aid budget constraints, and enrollment management factors all shape outcomes that families have no visibility into. No offers does not mean your child was found inadequate. Schools don't reject five-year-olds. They build classes.
>
> **How common is it:** It happens to families who did everything correctly — who applied to a thoughtful list, prepared appropriately, and showed up fully. Bay Area parents who share experiences on forums like Berkeley Parents Network report that full-cycle rejections often come as shocks to families with excellent applications. This does not mean the process is broken. It means the process is genuinely competitive and the outcomes are not fully in anyone's control.
>
> **What you can do now:** Several concrete things. Immediately, in April, and over the summer. They are listed below.
>
> **What not to over-interpret:** A no-offer cycle is not a prediction about your child's future, their academic ability, or their fit for private school. Many families who receive no offers in one cycle are admitted in a subsequent cycle. Many children who attend public K go on to thrive in private middle schools or high schools. The March 19 outcome does not determine the rest of your child's educational life.

### What to do immediately

**Apply to Gideon Hausner's second round** (by April 16) if Hausner is a fit. This is your fastest real option.

**Apply to Challenger or Stratford.** Both accept applications now, and both run their own admissions processes independent of ISSFBA. Placement assessments are administered in-house; the timelines are weeks, not months.

**Call your public school district's enrollment office.** If you haven't enrolled yet, do so now. Your child is guaranteed a kindergarten seat in your public school district — not necessarily the specific school you'd choose within the district, but a seat. That seat exists. It is yours.

### Medium-term: public school for K, then reapply

If the immediate options above don't land, the path most admissions advisors and experienced parents recommend is: enroll in public K, and reapply for first grade or for the following K cycle.

The first-grade entry point exists at many Bay Area private schools, and it is often less competitive than K entry because the applicant pool is smaller. Schools do have first-grade openings. Families who applied for K and were not admitted have been admitted a year later. No data exists on reapplication rates — but the pattern is real enough that advisors consistently recommend it to families who want to persist.

If you choose to reapply, think clearly about whether you want to apply to the same schools or different ones. If the mismatch was mission-based — the schools you applied to genuinely weren't the right environment for your child — a different list may serve you better. If the mismatch was compositional (class balance, financial aid, sibling spots), the same schools are worth revisiting. Your preschool director is often a useful sounding board here; some preschool directors have relationships with admissions directors and can provide informal context about what happened.

### Feedback from admissions offices

Some admissions offices will offer a brief conversation after March decisions. Many will not — this is not a slight, it reflects both policy and capacity constraints during a period when admissions staff are managing hundreds of families simultaneously.

If an office does offer a conversation, take it. Take notes. Ask open-ended questions: "Is there anything about our application or timing that you'd suggest we approach differently in a future cycle?" Don't argue with the answer. Don't ask for a specific reason the offer wasn't extended. Use what they share to think clearly about your next step.

## The Emotional Architecture

This is a long paragraph about what to do with your feelings. It earns its place.

Every scenario in this chapter — including admission — carries an emotional weight that doesn't dissolve when the email arrives. The admitted family feels relief and then, quickly, the pressure of choice. The waitlisted family swings between hope and dread for weeks. The family with no offers faces something that can feel like failure, even when they understand rationally that it isn't.

None of those feelings are wrong. The process is genuinely hard. It asks parents to assess their children, evaluate institutions, spend significant money and time on a process with uncertain outcomes, and then wait — which is often the hardest part.

What to do with the feelings practically: don't make major decisions on March 19. Don't accept or decline anything in the first hour except to write down what you received. Don't call every friend you have to report the result. Give yourself an evening. Then turn to the decision tree.

If you're waitlisted and the uncertainty is genuinely affecting your wellbeing or your child's sense of stability, that is a sign to prioritize confirming a school — any school — so that your child's fall has a settled answer. The waitlist matters less than your child's sense that the year ahead is solid.

If you received no offers and are in genuine distress: you are not alone in this experience, the options above are real, and March 19 is not the verdict. It is a data point. One data point.

## Multiple-Offer Comparison Framework

If you're holding more than one offer and the choice isn't obvious, these are the questions that admissions advisors and experienced Bay Area parents report actually predict family satisfaction — ordered by what tends to matter most.

**1. Which environment fits how your child actually is — not how you hope they'll be?**
This is a question about your child's temperament, not their potential. A child who thrives with structured routines and clear expectations and a school that prizes open-ended self-directed exploration can both be excellent. The question is match, not quality.

**2. Which school community can you genuinely participate in for seven or more years?**
You're not just enrolling your child — you're joining a parent community, a fundraising culture, an event calendar. The families at the spring fair matter. So does the commute. So does the fundraising expectation. Are these communities you'd actually want to spend time in?

**3. What is the real net cost of each school?**
Subtract aid from tuition. Add expected annual giving, school fees, and uniform costs if applicable. If the difference between two schools is $8,000 per year, that is a real consideration — not a values failure.

**4. What does your gut say about who your child was on campus?**
You observed your child during tours and playdates. One school may have produced a version of your child you recognized as them at their best. That observation is not nothing.

**5. Which school does your preschool director know?**
This is an underused question. Preschool directors often have relationships with K admissions staff and an informed sense of fit. "Which of these schools do you think will be a good fit for [child]?" is a direct question worth asking if you have a trusted relationship.
