# Chapter 11: Special Situations — A Guide for Families Who Don't Fit the Standard Path

---

Most of this book assumes you are applying for a typical kindergartener on a standard timeline. But what if you're not? What if your child has a late birthday, or a learning difference, or you're applying from outside the Bay Area, or you want a Spanish immersion school, or you're starting the process in January, or you're a single parent navigating this alone?

The standard advice still applies to your situation — but it doesn't cover the parts that are different. This chapter is not a linear narrative. It is a set of self-contained mini-guides. Find your situation, read that section. The rest you can skip.

## Section 1: The Late Birthday and the TK Question

### What's different about this situation

California law sets the standard kindergarten cutoff at September 1: a child must turn 5 by that date to enroll in kindergarten. But children with birthdays between September 2 and December 2 have an additional option — Transitional Kindergarten.

According to the California Department of Education, TK is the first year of a two-year kindergarten program. Starting in 2025–26, California public TK is available to all children who turn four by September 1, and local educational agencies are required to offer it. Public TK is free.

That's the public path. The private path is different: several Bay Area private schools offer their own TK programs, each with its own age cutoffs, tuition, and relationship to kindergarten admission at that school.

### What the standard advice doesn't cover

The TK decision for late-birthday children involves three genuinely distinct questions that the standard process chapter doesn't address:

**Public TK vs. private TK vs. waiting a year.** Public TK is free and guaranteed. Private TK — at schools like Harker (San Jose), Synapse (Menlo Park), Park Day (Oakland), and Stratford — carries tuition that is generally comparable to kindergarten tuition at the same school. Stratford's TK rate in 2025–26 was $25,170, matching its K rate. Harker's TK is $46,350 annually.

**The private TK pipeline question.** Community sources often describe private TK programs as internal pipelines to kindergarten at the same school. Multiple sources point to Harker, Synapse, and CAIS as having this structure. Harker's official admissions language describes TK as providing "an additional year of academic/social growth before transitioning into kindergarten the following year" — but does not explicitly state that TK students receive admission priority for K. No school publicly confirms this as policy. The pipeline is inferred from program structure, not official commitment.

What this means in practice: private TK may give your child a meaningful familiarity advantage and internal visibility at that school, but it is not a guarantee of K admission. Do not enroll in private TK at a school you don't actually want to attend for K.

**Redshirting: should you wait a year?** Redshirting — holding a child out of kindergarten until age six — is a genuine option for late-birthday children who are not ready. The research on early vs. late kindergarten entry is mixed. Some studies suggest that older entrants have short-term academic advantages that largely disappear by third or fourth grade. Others suggest that late-birthday children in academically demanding programs benefit from the extra year of development. Admissions advisors who work with Bay Area families do not report a consensus view on redshirting; it is a developmental question, not a strategic one.

### What to do

1. Confirm your child's eligibility. If your child's birthday is September 2 through December 2, they may be eligible for public TK. If it is September 1 or earlier, they are eligible for public kindergarten.

2. If considering private TK, apply to private TK programs using the standard admissions process — they are typically as competitive as K admissions. Do not assume TK is an easier back door.

3. Consult your child's preschool director or a developmental pediatrician if you are genuinely uncertain whether your child is ready for kindergarten. The readiness question should drive the timing decision — not admissions strategy.

4. If you're applying to Harker specifically: Harker's age cutoff for TK is more flexible than its K cutoff (November 30 vs. September 1). A child who doesn't quite qualify for K may qualify for TK, which may lead to K — but plan for this as a two-year investment.

## Section 2: Applying for Language Immersion

### What's different about this situation

The Bay Area has a genuine cluster of language immersion private schools at the K level. Verified programs include:

- **CAIS** (Mandarin, San Francisco) — K tuition $46,300 for 2026–27; deadline January 16, 2026
- **La Scuola International School** (Italian, San Francisco and Silicon Valley) — K tuition $48,850 SF / $39,775 SV for 2026–27; deadline January 15, 2026
- **Lycee Francais de San Francisco** (French, San Francisco and Marin) — tuition approximately $35,900–$45,900 for 2025–26; rolling admissions, first notifications in early March
- **Silicon Valley International School / SVIS** (French, Mandarin, or German, Palo Alto) — K tuition $42,630 for 2025–26; deadline January 13, 2026

Terra School (SF, trilingual) and East Bay German International School (German, East Bay) are sometimes mentioned but had not been independently verified as of this writing.

The most important fact for families without existing language knowledge: all four verified programs explicitly do not require prior knowledge of the target language at kindergarten entry. Immersion is the method of instruction, not an admissions screen.

### What the standard advice doesn't cover

**The Lycee Francais grade-level offset.** This is the most common planning error for families considering French immersion. Lycee Francais CP — the French equivalent of kindergarten — requires children to be 6 years old by September 1. A child turning 5 by September 1 (the typical US K cutoff) would enter La Petite Ecole or Petite Section, not CP. CP corresponds roughly to US first grade.

This matters for school-list comparisons: a child who would be in kindergarten at SF Day School would be in a different program tier at Lycee. Plan accordingly when comparing programs, and confirm directly with Lycee's admissions office how this affects your child's specific placement.

**Different platforms.** SVIS and Lycee Francais use different application platforms than the Ravenna network that handles most SF private schools. Lycee uses Veracross. SVIS has its own portal. Neither school is part of the ISSFBA. Their timelines, response windows, and admissions cultures are distinct. Treat each as a separate process.

**Maintaining immersion at home.** Schools do not screen for this, but once enrolled it matters. Admissions consultants who work with Bay Area immersion families consistently advise that families who take immersion seriously — seeking out books, media, and playdates in the target language — see faster acquisition. This is post-enrollment guidance, not an admissions requirement, but it's useful to think about now: are you prepared to support this long-term?

### What to do

1. Apply through each school's specific platform — do not assume these schools are in Ravenna.

2. For Lycee Francais: clarify with admissions which grade level your child would enter, based on their September 1 age. Do not compare "kindergarten" at Lycee to "kindergarten" elsewhere without accounting for this.

3. For SVIS: if you are uncertain which language track to choose, SVIS is the only school offering three tracks (French, Mandarin, German) at one campus, which may simplify your decision.

4. Use the same playdate preparation guidance as for non-immersion schools. No language screen happens at the playdate; evaluators are observing the same developmental dimensions — curiosity, engagement, social readiness — as at any observation-based school.

## Section 3: Learning Differences and Disclosure

> **Note:** This section addresses general patterns reported by admissions advisors and community sources. It is not legal advice, and situations vary significantly by child, school, and diagnosis. Consult an education attorney, advocate, or educational psychologist for guidance specific to your child's situation.

### What's different about this situation

Families whose children have a documented learning difference, IEP, or 504 plan face a question the standard application process does not directly address: do you need to disclose this, and if so, when and how?

The legal starting point matters: private schools in California are not subject to the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA) in the same way public schools are. Public schools are legally required to provide a free and appropriate public education, including specialized services documented in an IEP. Private schools have no such obligation. Some private schools offer meaningful learning support; others offer very little; a few are genuinely not equipped to serve children with certain learning profiles. This is not a reason to avoid private school — but it is a reason to evaluate carefully.

### What the standard advice doesn't cover

**Do you have to disclose?** Bay Area private K applications typically do not require parents to disclose a diagnosis, IEP, or 504 plan. Admissions advisors who work with Bay Area families report that disclosure is almost always voluntary at the K level. The "Further Information" section of many applications is an optional field — you are not omitting required information if you leave it blank.

**Should you disclose?** Advisors vary on this, and the right answer depends on your child, the specific learning difference, and the school's support capacity. A few considerations:

- If your child's profile would be apparent in a playdate — for example, if your child has significant language or sensory differences that a trained evaluator will observe — disclosure before the playdate allows the school to contextualize what they see. Without context, an evaluator may simply note the behavior without understanding it.
- If your child's learning difference is managed well and unlikely to be visible in a one-hour group session, disclosure becomes a school-fit question rather than a transparency obligation.
- Admissions consultants who work with Bay Area families report that some schools respond to disclosure with genuine interest and inquiry; others respond by quietly deprioritizing the application. The school's response to your disclosure tells you something important about fit.

**Evaluating a school's support model honestly.** "We have a learning support team" covers a wide range from a part-time resource teacher to a fully developed program with individualized learning plans. Advisors consistently recommend asking specific questions: How many children in the current kindergarten class have learning differences? What does your learning support team look like — how many staff, how many days per week? What happens when a child needs more support than the school can provide? A school that has thoughtful, specific answers is a different school from one that offers reassurance without substance.

**The "Further Information" section.** If you choose to disclose, this is where to do it. Advisors recommend being factual and forward-looking: describe the diagnosis, what your child has access to now, what progress looks like, and what support you're looking for from a school. Avoid framing that sounds like you're asking for reassurance; frame it as information the school needs to assess fit honestly.

### What to do

1. Before deciding whether to disclose, make a list of the specific supports your child uses and needs. That list is the foundation of any disclosure conversation.

2. For each school on your list, ask specific (not general) questions about learning support capacity. Do this before you decide whether to disclose, so you know which schools are genuinely equipped.

3. If you disclose in the application, do so in the "Further Information" section. Keep it factual, specific, and forward-looking.

4. Do not rely on a private school's stated willingness to "work with your child" as a substitute for asking what that means concretely. The question is not whether they're open to it — the question is whether they have the staff and experience to do it well.

5. Families who ultimately enroll in public kindergarten while continuing to evaluate private options for later grades can access IEP services through public school in the interim. This is a path some Bay Area families take intentionally.

## Section 4: Applying from Outside the Bay Area

### What's different about this situation

If your family is relocating to the Bay Area, you are running a standard admissions process under two additional constraints: you may not be physically present for events that most Bay Area families attend in person, and your timeline is tied to relocation uncertainty in a way that most families don't face.

### What the standard advice doesn't cover

**Virtual open houses exist and have improved.** Most Bay Area private schools now offer virtual open house options, particularly since 2020–21. You can complete information sessions and general open houses remotely in most cases. This removes one barrier, but not all of them.

**Playdates are almost always in person.** The assessment playdate — the observation session where evaluators watch your child in a group setting — is conducted on campus. Most schools will not conduct remote playdates. This means you need to plan for at least one in-person trip to the Bay Area during the January–February window, when playdates occur. If you have multiple schools on your list, you may need to coordinate multiple visits.

**Schools want to know about the relocation.** In both your parent essay and interview, being clear about your relocation plans matters. Schools want to understand: Where are you now, why are you moving to the Bay Area, when do you expect to arrive, and how certain is the timeline? A family that says "we expect to move in July" is in a different position than one that says "we'll move when our house sells." Honesty about uncertainty is better than overpromising a timeline that may slip.

Admissions consultants who work with relocating families consistently recommend framing the relocation positively and specifically — what draws your family to the area, why now, what you know about the neighborhood where you'll live. A sense that you've done your homework about the Bay Area is more reassuring to a school than generic enthusiasm.

**Start earlier than local families.** The standard admissions calendar starts in August–September. Relocating families should start their school research in June–July at the latest. You need extra lead time to identify schools, research neighborhoods in parallel with school research, and build relationship with admissions offices who may need to accommodate your remote status.

### What to do

1. Begin school research 2–3 months before the typical local start — June or July, not September.

2. Contact each school's admissions office early and directly: introduce your family, explain the relocation, and ask what their process accommodates for out-of-area families. Most will tell you clearly what is virtual-eligible and what is not.

3. Plan for at least one in-person trip during the playdate window (January–February). If possible, schedule multiple playdates in a single Bay Area visit.

4. In your application essays and parent interview, address the relocation directly. Be specific about timing, about what draws you to this area, and about any uncertainty you have.

5. If your relocation timeline is genuinely uncertain, consider which schools have more flexible or rolling admissions policies — Challenger School and Stratford School, for example, admit on a rolling basis and may be more accommodating than schools with strict January deadline cycles.

## Section 5: The Gifted Child at Observation-Based Schools

### What's different about this situation

Most Bay Area private K schools — particularly in San Francisco — use observation-based playdates, not IQ testing, to assess applicants. A handful of schools (Nueva, Helios, Harker) require formal IQ testing and specifically seek children with high cognitive scores. If your child is clearly gifted and you're applying to observation-based schools, the process isn't structured to elicit or reward that specifically.

This is more feature than bug — but it requires a different kind of preparation and a different kind of parent essay.

### What the standard advice doesn't cover

**Evaluators will observe curiosity and engagement — which happens to serve many gifted children well.** Admissions consulting sources who have observed Bay Area playdate formats report that observation-based schools commonly assess whether a child can follow instructions, engage with materials, participate in a group, and communicate clearly. A gifted child who is genuinely curious, asks interesting questions, and engages persistently with a problem will tend to demonstrate those qualities naturally. For many such children, the observation format is not a structural disadvantage.

What can sometimes be a disadvantage: gifted children who are bored by simple tasks, frustrated by group pacing, or perfectionistic about materials can display behaviors at a playdate — shutting down, refusing to engage, becoming agitated — that evaluators observe without the context of why.

**You don't need to flag giftedness in the application — unless you do.** For most children with high cognitive ability who are otherwise developmentally typical, there is no need to mention giftedness in the standard parent essay. Describe your child specifically and honestly — their persistence, their curiosity, their particular interests. Let the qualities speak.

The case for raising it directly: if your child's giftedness is pronounced enough that it shapes their daily behavior in ways that might appear in a playdate, it's worth addressing — either in the essay or in a direct pre-application conversation with the admissions office. A child who can read chapter books at four, or who becomes deeply frustrated when prevented from completing a self-set task, benefits from the evaluator having that context.

**Writing about a gifted child without sounding like you're bragging.** Admissions consultants who work with Bay Area families consistently flag this as a specific risk. The parent essay is not the place for developmental milestone lists. The essay that says "he was reading by age three, has a vocabulary his preschool teachers describe as remarkable, and completed a 100-piece puzzle independently at 36 months" reads as advocacy, not introduction. The essay that describes what it's like to have a conversation with him — the way he follows a question to unexpected places, the things he wonders about at dinner — does the same work more persuasively.

**When it's worth a direct conversation.** If your child's intellectual profile is significant enough that you genuinely wonder whether an observation-based school is the right fit, that is itself useful information. Ask the admissions director directly: given what I've described about our child, does your program typically serve children at this level well? A school that has seen this before will answer thoughtfully.

### What to do

1. Prepare your child for playdate formats by ensuring they're comfortable in group settings and can pace themselves through simple tasks without frustration — not by drilling cognitive skills.

2. Write the parent essay around specific, concrete scenes that reveal character. Avoid developmental milestone lists.

3. If your child's profile is pronounced, consider whether to have a direct pre-application conversation with the admissions office about fit.

4. If giftedness is the primary driver of your school search, the IQ-testing schools (Nueva, Helios, Harker) have programs explicitly designed for high-ability learners. Those are covered in Chapter 4.

## Section 6: Single-Parent and Non-Traditional Families

### What's different about this situation

Bay Area private K admissions events, interviews, and application materials are built around certain default assumptions — two parents, paired attendance, a family structure that maps onto school events designed for two adults. If your family doesn't fit that structure, you may find yourself navigating logistics and framing questions the standard guidance doesn't address.

### What the standard advice doesn't cover

**The parent interview.** The standard parent interview format involves both parents meeting with an admissions director or small group. If you're a single parent, or if your co-parent cannot attend, or if your family structure is complex, this is worth addressing directly.

Admissions consultants who work with diverse Bay Area families report that schools are generally comfortable with one-parent interviews — Bay Area private schools, particularly in San Francisco, enroll families with a wide range of structures. If you're coming alone, you don't need to apologize for it or explain it in the interview itself. Lead with your child and your values.

If co-parenting is part of your picture and both co-parents have a role in the child's education but won't both be at the interview, admissions consultants suggest briefly acknowledging this at the start — "I'll be speaking for our family today; [co-parent] is also engaged in [child's] education but wasn't able to join" — and then moving directly into the conversation. Don't over-explain family structure; focus on your child.

**The parent essay.** The essay asks about your family — its educational values, how you approach challenges, what community means to you. You don't owe any school a detailed account of your family's structure or history. Describe the family your child actually lives in, using the language your family uses, without either hiding it or foregrounding it. An essay about a single parent and child that centers on how you navigate curiosity, reading together, and building resilience is as strong as any essay with two parents in it.

**School events.** Some schools organize events — fundraisers, field trips, parent committee work — in ways that implicitly assume two-parent availability. This is a practical logistics consideration, not an admissions concern, but it's worth factoring into how you evaluate whether a school's community culture is actually a fit for your family. Ask current single parents at schools on your list how the community works for them.

### What to do

1. At the parent interview, there is no requirement that both parents attend. If you're coming alone, a brief, confident acknowledgment is sufficient — no extended explanation needed.

2. In the parent essay, describe your family as it actually is. You do not need to center the essay on family structure; write about your child, your values, and your reasons for applying.

3. When evaluating school fit, ask the admissions office about the current family community — what percentage of families have a single parent, or non-traditional structure? Schools in the Bay Area vary widely in how diverse their parent communities actually are.

4. Bay Area private schools, particularly in San Francisco, generally have experience with diverse family structures — this is consistent across what admissions consultants and families in these communities report. Whether this is true at any specific school on your list is worth confirming directly. Asking the admissions office about the current composition of their parent community is a reasonable question and will tell you more than a general assumption.

## Section 7: Starting the Process Late

### What's different about this situation

The standard admissions calendar assumes you start in August–September, complete open houses in fall, and submit applications in December–January ahead of coordinated March notifications. If it is now January or later, you have missed the primary deadlines for most ISSFBA schools. That is a real constraint — but it does not mean your options are gone.

### What the standard advice doesn't cover

**What's still realistically available after December deadlines.** Two categories of schools remain accessible through spring:

*Rolling-admissions schools* accept applications year-round based on space availability. Challenger School and Stratford School — both with multiple Bay Area campuses — operate this way. These are strong schools, not fallback options. Challenger and Stratford use their own application processes outside the ISSFBA framework, which is exactly why they don't have the same January deadlines.

*Schools with a second round.* Gideon Hausner Jewish Day School (Palo Alto) has historically had a Round 2 admissions process in April for families who apply after the primary deadline. This is worth checking directly with the school for the current cycle.

**Can you still apply to December-deadline ISSFBA schools in January?** For most ISSFBA schools, the answer is: probably not for this cycle. After January deadlines close, schools move into application review and are not accepting new applications. However, it is worth a direct call to any school on your list where you genuinely want to be considered — some schools with smaller applicant pools than anticipated may have informal flexibility. Ask directly, briefly, and without assuming they will accommodate.

**You are not locked out of the next cycle.** If you're starting too late for this cycle, the best use of your time is to treat this year as preparation: attend open houses at schools that admit visitors even post-deadline, build your list, and start the actual application process in August.

### What to do

1. Call Challenger School and Stratford School now. Both have multiple Bay Area campuses and rolling admissions. Ask about current availability for the fall.

2. Check directly with Gideon Hausner about their April Round 2 process.

3. For any ISSFBA school that remains on your list, make a brief, direct call to admissions. Ask specifically whether late applications are being considered. Accept the answer gracefully either way.

4. For any school you cannot reach this cycle: request to be added to their mailing list and notification for when next cycle's open houses begin. Most schools open open house registration in August; that is when your actual process begins.

## Section 8: Reapplying After a Previous Cycle

### What's different about this situation

You've done this before. You know how the process works. That experience is an asset — but it also comes with specific questions the first-time applicant doesn't face: Is it different the second time? Should you use the same school list? What do you do when an interviewer asks if you applied before?

### What the standard advice doesn't cover

**Is it actually different the second time?** Often yes — but not because the school suddenly sees you differently. It's different because your child is a year older. A child who was evaluated at four years and seven months is now five years and seven months. That developmental gap is significant. The child who struggled to settle into a group playdate a year ago may be genuinely different now. Bay Area admissions consultants and community sources report that schools genuinely re-evaluate returning applicants rather than treating the previous application as a ceiling. Some families who were not admitted in a first cycle are admitted on reapplication; admissions consultants who work with Bay Area families consistently cite developmental change — a child who is a year older is meaningfully different — as the most common factor they observe in these cases. This is advisor-reported pattern, not school-published data.

**Should you use the same school list?** Partly. Some schools make sense to revisit; others are worth adding. A useful reapplication framework:

- Keep schools where the fit was genuine and the evaluation observation was positive even if the outcome wasn't.
- Consider adding schools you didn't include in the first cycle, particularly if you are willing to expand geography or philosophy.
- Drop schools where you've learned, through conversations with current families or admissions advisors, that the community is unlikely to be a fit for your child's specific profile.

Reapplication is also a good moment to ask your preschool director for candid feedback — if they spoke with admissions directors after the first cycle, they may have insight into what evaluators observed.

**When the school knows you applied before.** Most schools keep records. If you are reapplying to the same school, assume they know this. In the parent interview, the standard guidance from admissions advisors who work with Bay Area families is to be direct and positive: acknowledge that you applied before, describe what has changed — in your child, and in your family's understanding of what this school offers — and explain why you are returning. A family that comes back with greater clarity and genuine commitment reads very differently from a family that reapplies without having learned anything from the first cycle.

**Reapplication success.** Some parents on Berkeley Parents Network report that families have been admitted on a second K application or a subsequent-grade application to the same school. No school publishes reapplication success rates, and no Bay Area-specific data exists. Treat community accounts as illustrative, not statistical.

### What to do

1. Consult your preschool director before deciding which schools to reapply to. They may have information from the first cycle that helps you calibrate.

2. Update your parent essay fully — do not resubmit last year's version. Your child has changed; the essay should reflect that.

3. If reapplying to the same school, address the previous application in the interview directly and briefly, then move forward. Don't lead with apology or over-explanation.

4. Use the first cycle's experience to tighten your school list — fewer schools where the fit is genuinely strong, rather than the same broad list applied again.

5. Consider adding one rolling-admissions school (Challenger or Stratford) to your list as a confirmed option, regardless of how strong your revised list is. Having a known landing spot changes how you navigate the rest of the process.
