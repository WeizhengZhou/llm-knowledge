# Chapter 6: Recommendation Letters — The Part You Think Is Out of Your Hands

---

Most parents approaching the recommendation letter process believe it sits entirely outside their control. The teacher writes what the teacher writes. You ask nicely, say thank you, and hope for the best.

That understanding is partly right — and partly exactly backwards.

What the teacher writes is genuinely not yours to direct. That's not just an admissions norm; it's an explicit rule enforced by schools and advisors alike. But when you ask, how you ask, how much context you give the relationship to develop, and how you manage the logistics around submission — all of that is entirely within your control. And in a process where parents feel buffeted by things they can't influence, the recommendation letter is one of the few parts of the application where timing and logistics are genuinely in your hands.

This chapter explains what the form actually covers, why timing is the highest-leverage thing you can affect, and what you can and cannot do to support the teacher who will write it.

---

## What the Form Actually Is

Most families applying to Bay Area private schools will not be managing separate recommendation letters for each school. They will be managing one form.

The ISSFBA Confidential Recommendation Form is a standardized evaluation document that routes through the Ravenna Hub platform to all ISSFBA member schools on your list simultaneously. Your preschool teacher fills it out once; it goes wherever you've applied. This is a significant convenience — both for you and for the teacher, who doesn't need to write ten variations of the same letter.

A few important specifics about the form:

- It is submitted directly by the teacher through Ravenna or the school's portal — **not** through you. You cannot see it before it's submitted. It is 100% confidential.
- It covers the same dimensions that school evaluators look for in playdates: how the child interacts with peers, manages frustration, approaches new challenges, communicates, and navigates the classroom community.
- Non-ISSFBA schools on your list may have their own separate forms or request processes. Check each school's admissions page individually. Schools like Marin Country Day School (not on Ravenna for all requests) may route requests differently.

For IQ-testing schools — Nueva, Helios, Harker — teacher evaluations are a component of the application alongside the cognitive assessment. The same principles apply; the form may differ.

## The September Signal: Why Timing Is Everything

Most parents make the formal recommendation request in November. Admissions advisors who work regularly with Bay Area families consistently note a pattern: the teachers who write the most specific, observational letters are often ones who had months of application-aware context to draw from — not weeks.

Here is what experienced admissions advisors consistently recommend, and why:

In September, you are not asking the teacher to write anything. You are having a brief, warm, informational conversation: "We're going to be applying to private K this cycle, and we'll come back to formally ask in November. Just wanted you to know, so you have the full picture as you're getting to know [child] this fall."

That's it. That's the whole September conversation.

Why this matters: a teacher who knows an application cycle is coming will observe your child with a different lens. Not a more favorable lens — the teacher isn't going to inflate their assessment because you mentioned it. But a more attentive lens. They'll notice the moment your child comforts a classmate who's upset. They'll remember the afternoon your child spent forty minutes rebuilding a structure that kept falling. They'll have specific, concrete material to draw from when November comes.

A teacher who is asked in December — during the busiest professional period of their year, often amid report cards, parent conferences, and holiday events — is writing about the past three months from memory, in a compressed window. In advisors' experience, letters written under that time pressure tend to be less specific than ones written by someone who had more runway.

**What the September conversation looks like:**

> "We'll be applying to private K this year and will circle back in November to ask more formally. No action needed now — just wanted you to be aware."

Brief. Low-pressure. No forms, no lists, no additional information required from the teacher. Just a heads-up that sets the stage.

## November: The Formal Ask

By November, you've been to open houses, you have a working school list, and you know your December and January deadlines. This is when the formal request happens.

**Ask in person when possible.** A face-to-face conversation carries more weight than an email request. It communicates that you value the teacher's time and see them as a genuine partner in this process, not a checkbox to complete. Admissions advisors consistently flag this: the in-person ask matters.

**Contact the teacher before submitting forms.** This is one of the most commonly missed steps in the process. Ravenna allows you to send recommendation form requests directly to teachers from the portal — and many parents, understandably, just click send. Don't. Reach out first, have the conversation, get a yes, and then send the electronic request. A teacher who receives a Ravenna notification without warning may feel blindsided; one who was expecting it is ready.

**What to bring to the November conversation:**

- A clear explanation of which schools you're applying to
- The specific deadlines for each school (more on this below)
- A brief, genuine explanation of why this teacher is the right person to write the form — not flattery, but specificity. "You've seen [child] navigate the classroom in a way that other adults in their life haven't."
- A proposed internal deadline for submission: mid-December, which gives buffer before the holiday break and before the first December school deadlines

**What not to bring:** talking points, a list of things to highlight, a summary of your child's strengths. More on this below.

## What Makes a Strong Letter

You won't write the letter. But you can understand what strong ones contain — and that understanding shapes whether the relationship you've built gives the teacher material to work with.

The most useful recommendations, according to admissions consultants who review applications and debrief with school admissions offices, share a structural quality: **they are specific and observational, not general and complimentary.**

A weak letter reads: "She is a wonderful child who loves learning and gets along well with everyone."

A strong letter reads: "When the class was introduced to a new math game last month, she spent several minutes watching before joining. When she did join, she asked whether there was a faster way to add up the scores. She was right, and she explained it to the other children without being asked."

The difference is not literary quality. It's observational specificity. An admissions reader who has reviewed hundreds of applications can tell the difference between a teacher describing a real child they know and a teacher writing a generic positive assessment.

What strong letters typically cover — based on what advisors report evaluators find most useful:

- **How the child approaches challenge.** Does the child try different strategies, ask for help at the right moments, recover from frustration?
- **How the child interacts with peers.** A leader in the formal sense is less interesting than a child who is kind, who notices when others are struggling, who can play alongside children with different styles.
- **How the child engages with learning.** Curiosity, persistence, the quality of attention they bring to difficult material.
- **What the child's challenges are.** A teacher who can name one or two genuine areas of growth — without making them sound disqualifying — signals to admissions readers that this is an honest, complete picture. Schools that are reading the letter know what they're looking for; a purely positive letter raises its own questions.

*This reflects patterns advisors consistently report from working with Bay Area families and debriefing with school admissions offices. Schools do not publish their internal rubrics for evaluating letters.*

## What Parents Should Not Do

This section exists because the impulse to be helpful runs directly into a hard rule.

Schools are explicit: the teacher recommendation form is **100% confidential** and must represent the teacher's own professional perspective. According to the guidance published by established admissions consulting resources and confirmed by school policies, parents should not:

- Provide bullet points of what to highlight about their child
- Send the teacher a summary of the child's strengths or special qualities
- Share anecdotes they hope will appear in the letter
- Ask to review the form before it is submitted
- Give the teacher a "sense of what schools are looking for"

The concern is not that parents are trying to cheat. The concern is that a teacher writing from parent-provided talking points is no longer providing an independent professional assessment — which is the entire point of the form. Admissions consultants who work with Bay Area families consistently report that experienced admissions readers can often tell the difference between a teacher's own observations and a letter assembled from parent-supplied material. Letters that closely echo what parents have also written elsewhere in an application tend to read as less independent, and therefore less useful.

**The right answer to wanting to help:** build the relationship in September. Keep your child present, curious, and engaged in the classroom. Let the teacher observe what they observe. Trust that a teacher who genuinely knows your child will write a genuine letter.

**One thing you can appropriately do:** let the teacher know, briefly, that a particular dimension of your child isn't visible in the classroom. "She tends to be quieter at school than at home — just wanted you to have the full picture." This is contextual information, not a direction to write about something specific. Say it once, briefly, without suggesting what you'd like the teacher to do with it. The line between providing context and nudging toward talking points is worth staying clearly on the right side of.

## Navigating Special Situations

### My child has only one teacher

The ISSFBA form is designed for this common situation. One letter from the primary preschool teacher covers all Ravenna schools. If a school requests a second letter and your child has had only one teacher, a previous year's teacher — someone who observed the child in an earlier classroom context — can supplement. Some schools accept a second letter from a coach, activity leader, or trusted adult who has worked with the child in a structured group setting. Check each school's specific requirement.

### We had a difficult year with this teacher

This is a harder situation, and it warrants honesty in how you handle it. A strained teacher relationship doesn't automatically produce a weak letter — teachers are professionals, and most can write an honest assessment of a child's classroom functioning even amid parent tension. But if you have reason to believe the relationship will genuinely affect the letter's quality, you have a few options: ask a previous teacher to write the primary form, or speak directly with the admissions director at a school you're particularly interested in, explain the situation briefly and professionally, and ask their guidance. Most admissions offices have seen this before.

### My child has a documented speech delay or learning difference

If there's information in the letter that requires context — a speech delay that affects how the teacher describes verbal communication, or a processing difference that shows up in classroom observation — it's worth flagging in your application's "Further Information" section, not by coaching the teacher. The admissions office reading the letter alongside your supplemental information is better positioned to interpret what they read accurately.

### Non-ISSFBA schools

Schools outside the ISSFBA network — Nueva, Harker, Helios, and others — will have their own recommendation processes. Some use Ravenna; some have their own portals; some request letters by email. Check each school's current admissions page. The advice on timing, the in-person ask, and the no-bullet-points rule applies equally.

## Deadlines — The Part Most Families Get Wrong

The recommendation form deadline is not the same as the application deadline. In many cases it is earlier — because the teacher needs time to complete the form, and it needs to arrive before the admissions office can process the application.

| School Type | Application Deadline | Recommendation Form Due |
|-------------|---------------------|-------------------------|
| December deadline schools (Hamlin, SF Friends, Convent & Stuart Hall, SF Day) | December 12–19 | Teacher should submit by early-to-mid December |
| January deadline schools (most ISSFBA) | January 5–30 (varies) | Teacher should submit before application deadline |
| Marin Country Day School | December 28 | Separate teacher evaluation forms: **February 1** |
| Non-ISSFBA schools (Nueva, Harker, Helios) | January 8–23 | Varies by school; check current admissions pages |

*Always verify current deadlines on each school's official admissions page. These reflect the 2025–26 cycle.*

**The December deadline wave** is the specific point where families get caught. If you've asked for the form in November and set a mid-December internal deadline, you should be fine. If you've asked in late November or December, you're asking a teacher to complete a form during the most compressed professional period of the year. The form may still get done. It may not be the teacher's best work.

**MCDS specifically:** Marin Country Day School's teacher evaluation forms are due February 1, separately from the December 28 application deadline. If MCDS is on your list, make sure your teacher knows this second deadline exists — it's easy to miss.

## Following Through

After the formal ask, two more moments matter.

**Before the holiday break:** Check in with the teacher to confirm the form has been submitted or is on track. Not a daily check — a single, polite inquiry. "Just wanted to make sure you have everything you need before the break." If you set a mid-December internal deadline, follow up in early December. Once it's submitted, send a thank-you.

**After all applications are submitted:** A written thank-you from you — and if your child can do it, from them — is the right close to this process. The teacher gave professional time and professional judgment to support your child's admissions. Acknowledging that, regardless of outcome, is both appropriate and remembered.

## Artifact: Your Recommendation Letter Script Guide

*These scripts reflect admissions advisor guidance for managing the teacher relationship well. Adapt to your own voice — the goal is genuine, not scripted.*

### The September Signal (in person or email)

> "I wanted to let you know that we'll be going through the private K admissions process this year. We'll come back to formally ask for a recommendation in November — no action needed now. Just wanted you to have that context as you're getting to know [child] this fall. Thank you for everything you do."

---

### The November Ask (in person first, then follow with email for the record)

> "We're ready to officially start the application process and would be grateful if you'd be willing to complete the ISSFBA recommendation form for [child]. You'll receive a request through [Ravenna / the school portal] — I wanted to reach out personally first.
>
> We're applying to [list schools]. The deadlines vary — the earliest is [date]. If it's possible to have the form in before [mid-December date], that would be ideal. I'll send the platform request right after this.
>
> Thank you for taking this on. We know it's a busy time of year."

---

### The Follow-Up (early December)

> "Just a quick note to see if you have what you need for the recommendation form before the holiday break. Happy to answer any questions. And thank you again — we really appreciate it."

---

### The Thank-You (after applications are submitted)

> "We've submitted our applications and wanted to thank you again for the time and thought you put into [child]'s recommendation. Whatever the outcome, we're grateful you were in their corner."
