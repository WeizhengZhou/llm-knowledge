# Chapter 3: What Schools Are Really Evaluating

---

Most parents, preparing for kindergarten admissions, imagine the schools are searching for something like a miniature prodigy. The child who already reads. Who counts past 100. Who sits still during a 20-minute story. Who performs.

They're not. And spending the next several months trying to produce that child — or worrying that yours doesn't measure up to it — is one of the most common and costly mistakes families make.

What schools are actually looking for is something both simpler and harder to manufacture: a child who is ready to learn in a group, to recover from small frustrations, to engage with something new, and to exist reasonably well alongside other four-year-olds. They're not ranking children by intelligence. They're asking one question: will this particular child thrive in this particular environment?

This chapter unpacks what that question actually means in practice — what evaluators observe, what they write down, and what they're genuinely not looking for. By the end, you should be able to assess your own child honestly against the dimensions that matter, and stop worrying about the ones that don't.

---

## Two Assessment Models: The Fork in the Road

Before getting into what evaluators look for, it's worth understanding that Bay Area private K schools use two fundamentally different assessment formats. Which model you're dealing with shapes everything.

**The playdate or observation model** is used by most San Francisco schools. Children are brought in for a supervised play session — sometimes in small groups, sometimes with brief individual interaction — where teachers and admissions staff observe them in a low-key environment. There is no test. There are no right answers. The setting is designed to feel like a structured morning at preschool. Children play with blocks, do a simple art activity, have a snack, and interact with unfamiliar adults and peers.

According to Cardinal Education, a Bay Area admissions consulting firm, the typical SF playdate involves "4-year-olds join a small group of kids for something like preschool circle time where teachers observe and interact, with snacks and free play in a low-key, low-pressure environment."

Schools using this format include Hamlin, SF Friends School, Live Oak School, Convent & Stuart Hall, La Scuola International, Presidio Hill, and The San Francisco School, among others. Most families applying to San Francisco schools will encounter only this model.

**The cognitive or IQ testing model** is used by a distinct group of gifted-focused schools, primarily on the Peninsula and in the South Bay. Children are assessed one-on-one by a licensed psychologist using a standardized instrument — typically the Wechsler Preschool and Primary Scale of Intelligence (WPPSI-IV). The session runs 45 minutes to two hours. Parents wait outside. This is covered in depth in Chapter 4.

Schools using this model include Nueva School (which requires a minimum FSIQ of approximately 130, according to the SF Standard), Helios School in Sunnyvale, and Harker School. Note that some schools use both formats: an IQ score for cognitive eligibility, and an observation-based component for social and behavioral fit.

The table below gives a snapshot of how key schools approach the assessment.

|--------|-------------------|----------|--------------------|

The remainder of this chapter focuses on the playdate model, which is what most SF families will navigate. If you're applying to Nueva, Helios, or Harker, read this chapter for the social-emotional dimensions (which still apply), then turn to Chapter 4 for everything specific to IQ testing.

> **What observation-based playdates are usually not primarily optimizing for:**
>
> - Above-grade-level academic performance — reading early, advanced counting, knowing the full alphabet on command
> - Confident performance in front of strangers
> - Perfect stillness or adult-level patience
> - Extroversion or talkativeness
>
> Some schools' assessment formats may include simple tasks — naming colors, counting a small set of objects, identifying some letters, writing a first name. But advisors consistently report that those are not the central differentiator in SF observation-based admissions. The evaluation is far broader than a readiness screening for basic academic skills.
>
> Admissions consultant Paula Molligan, widely quoted in Bay Area admissions discussions, put it directly: "There isn't one school that we know of that really cares or bothers to check whether a child is reading." That's a practitioner statement from someone who works closely with these schools — and it describes the specific concern parents most often over-index on.

## The 6 Dimensions — What Evaluators Actually Observe

There is no published rubric. No school hands out a checklist. But admissions consultants who work regularly with Bay Area families, and who debrief with school admissions offices over many years, have developed a consistent picture of what evaluators notice. The six dimensions below reflect that consensus. They are not school-confirmed policy — treat them as informed guidance about where to focus your attention, not as an official scoring system.

---

### 1. Social-Emotional Readiness

Advisors who work with Bay Area families consistently name this as the dimension that appears most prominently in evaluator notes — and it is the one most misunderstood by parents.

Social-emotional readiness does not mean a child who is perfectly socialized, endlessly patient, and never squabbles over a toy. It means something more specific: can this child exist productively in a group of unfamiliar children and unfamiliar adults?

Experienced advisors note that evaluators typically look for:

- Takes turns — imperfectly, with occasional reminders, but does it
- Uses words to express needs rather than grabbing, hitting, or shutting down
- Can recover from small frustrations. A toy taken. Losing a simple game. Not getting the color of crayon they wanted. Does the child spiral into an extended meltdown, or do they find their footing again within a few minutes?
- Warms to unfamiliar adults. Not immediately, necessarily. Evaluators understand that some children are slow to warm. But within a 60–90 minute session, does the child eventually engage?

What it does NOT mean: perfect sharing, zero tears, or adult-level patience. Four-year-olds grab toys. They cry at transitions. They want what they want loudly. Every evaluator in every SF school knows this, and none of them are looking for a child who doesn't behave like a four-year-old.

The behaviors that concern evaluators are at the extreme end — not the ordinary range. A child who is physically aggressive toward peers. A child who cannot function in the room at all without a parent present after 30 or more minutes. A child who is completely unable to engage with any adult or activity across the full session. These are not descriptions of most four-year-olds, including ones who are shy, active, or emotionally intense. Normal four-year-old behavior is not a red flag.

---

### 2. Communication and Language

What evaluators are watching for here is functional communication: the ability to express needs, answer questions, and sustain a back-and-forth exchange with both adults and peers.

Admissions consultants consistently report that evaluators notice:

- Speaks in sentences (not single words or gestures for everything)
- Responds to "what" and "why" questions — not necessarily with elaborate answers, but with an attempt
- Engages in two-way conversation, not just one-way broadcasting
- Can direct a comment or question to another child, not only to adults

What it does NOT mean: reading, writing, or a large vocabulary. A child who knows ten words for "dinosaur" is interesting, but a child who can say "I don't want to do that right now, I want the blocks" and have another child respond is demonstrating exactly what evaluators are looking for.

One practical note: if your child has a documented speech delay, this dimension is worth your attention before applications. It does not disqualify a child — many children with speech delays attend SF private schools — but it warrants honest handling. Address it directly in the application's "Further Information" section, and speak with your child's preschool teacher before interviews.

---

### 3. Curiosity and Engagement

Schools that use observation-based assessments are, in essence, small learning environments with a particular approach to education. What they are asking is: does this child want to learn here?

Evaluators notice whether a child:

- Approaches unfamiliar activities with interest rather than reluctance
- Spends time with an activity — building something at the block table, working through an art project — rather than bouncing from station to station every 90 seconds
- Asks questions about what they see or encounter
- Makes an attempt before asking for help or giving up

What it does NOT mean: advanced academic interest, reading readiness, or sustained attention beyond age expectations. A child who spends eight minutes building a block tower, knocks it down, and starts over is showing genuine engagement. A child who sits attentively through a 20-minute teacher-led activity is doing something four-year-olds generally cannot do. That's not what's being observed.

The distinction here matters especially for progressive SF schools, where the philosophy is child-led inquiry. A child who finds the snacks more interesting than the activities is fine. A child who shows genuine interest in nothing across a 90-minute visit may be having a bad day — or may genuinely not be a match for this environment.

---

### 4. Self-Regulation and Transitions

Four-year-olds have limited impulse control. Everyone knows this. What evaluators are watching for is not adult-level regulation — it's the beginning of it.

Admissions consultants who work with Bay Area families consistently report that evaluators notice whether a child can:

- Shift from one activity to another when a teacher signals it's time. "Okay, friends, let's clean up the blocks and move to the rug."
- Wait briefly for a turn without complete breakdown
- Participate in a short group activity — circle time, a simple game — for 10–15 minutes without continuous disruption

What it does NOT mean: instant compliance, perfect impulse control, or a child who never needs to be redirected. Teachers redirect four-year-olds constantly. That's part of the job. What evaluators are looking for is whether the child can, eventually, be redirected — whether there's some responsiveness to adult guidance.

Here's what this looks like in practice. A teacher announces it's time to clean up and move to the rug. One child immediately protests — "but I'm not done!" — and stays at the block station for another thirty seconds before reluctantly joining the group. That's a child showing self-regulation: the initial resistance, then the recovery, then the reintegration. An evaluator notes the arc. A child who protests the transition and is still standing at the block station, crying, fifteen minutes later — that's a different picture.

Transitions are a specific and predictable challenge for many children this age. If your child typically struggles with transitions, the most useful thing to know is that evaluators are watching for the arc, not the first moment.

---

### 5. Independence and Self-Care

This dimension is more practical than it sounds. Schools are assessing whether a child can function in a classroom setting without requiring constant adult support for basic physical needs.

Evaluators notice whether a child can:

- Manage bathroom independently
- Handle basic belongings — takes off a coat, puts things in a backpack
- Open containers (snack time reveals a lot about this)
- Ask an adult for help when they need it, rather than becoming distressed and waiting for someone to notice

What it does NOT mean: total self-sufficiency or never needing help. It means the child has enough basic independence that a teacher can attend to the whole group without one child requiring constant physical intervention.

The snack component of many SF playdates is not incidental. It's a structured moment where evaluators can observe a cluster of behaviors — independence, social conversation, patience — in a naturalistic context. Children who can open their snack, chat with the child next to them, and manage the transition back to activities are demonstrating multiple dimensions at once.

---

### 6. Problem-Solving and Adaptability

Of the six dimensions, this one tends to come up most often in conversations about why schools value what they value — because the next eight years of your child's education will require exactly these capacities.

Admissions consultants note that evaluators are watching whether a child:

- Tries a different approach when something doesn't work. A puzzle piece that won't fit. A block tower that keeps falling. A drawing that doesn't look the way they wanted.
- Can recover from small failures without extended distress
- Responds to a new or ambiguous challenge with interest rather than immediate shutdown

What this looks like at the behavioral level: a child who tries to put a puzzle piece in the wrong spot, pauses, rotates it, tries again. A child who builds a tower, watches it fall, says "again!" and starts rebuilding. A child who doesn't know what to do with a new material and asks another child, "what are you making with that?"

These behaviors signal cognitive flexibility and resilience — two qualities that predict learning success in almost any school environment. They are not signs of advanced intelligence. They are signs of a child who is ready to engage with challenge.

## What Evaluators Write Down

Here is something worth knowing: evaluators are not composing judgments. They are recording observations.

The note from a SF school playdate reads more like field notes than a report card. "Child spent approximately eight minutes at the block station, built a structure, noticed another child watching, said 'do you want to put something on it?' — Child took turns with verbal prompting. Needed approximately two minutes to settle after transition to snack."

This is specific and behavioral. Not "child is creative." Not "child has leadership potential." Not "child seems bright." Specific, observable, timed descriptions of what the child actually did.

This matters because it means your child's actual behavior — not your hopes or your preschool teacher's glowing recommendation — is what gets documented. It also means that a child who has a rough first ten minutes but engages well by the middle of the session gets credit for that arc. Evaluators are watching the full visit, not just the first impression.

And evaluators are not trying to rank children on a single dimension. They're asking: does this child seem like someone who will do well in our particular setting, with our particular teachers, using our particular approach? That's a fit question. A child who would thrive in a Montessori environment might not thrive in an inquiry-based progressive school, and vice versa. Neither child is wrong. Neither is found lacking. They're different.

## The Parent Interview

> **Note:** The parent interview is a separate evaluation. It runs alongside the child assessment and is weighted meaningfully. What's evaluated here is not your child's achievements — it's whether you and the school are a good fit for each other.

Many families think of the parent interview as a formality — a chance to ask questions and seem enthusiastic. It isn't. Admissions consultants who work with Bay Area families and maintain regular contact with school admissions teams report that parent interviews carry genuine weight. The patterns below reflect what advisors consistently observe across schools — they are not published evaluation criteria, and specific schools weight things differently. But the broad picture is consistent enough to be useful.

**What schools are actually evaluating:**

*Philosophy alignment.* Do you genuinely want what this school offers? Not "is this a prestigious school" — but do you understand how they teach and does that match what you want for your child? Schools can tell the difference between parents who have read the website and parents who have thought carefully about the school's approach and found it compelling.

*Realistic expectations.* Do you understand the school's model? Progressive schools field parents who want more structure; structured schools field parents who want more child-led exploration. Misalignment here is noticed and noted.

*Collaborative stance.* Will you work with teachers as partners, or push against them? Schools are assessing whether you'll be a constructive member of their community for the next decade. This shows up in how you respond to feedback, how you describe challenges, and whether you seem to listen.

*Honest self-awareness about your child.* Can you describe your child with both strengths and growth areas? Parents who can only offer praise — "she's brilliant, she's advanced, she loves to learn" — without acknowledging any dimension where their child is still developing, raise a flag. It signals either that the parent doesn't observe their child carefully, or that they're not willing to be honest. Neither bodes well for a long partnership.

**Sample questions schools ask** (these are sourced from admissions consultants, not published school policy — actual questions vary by school and interviewer):

- "Describe your child using three adjectives."
- "How does your family handle frustration or challenge?"
- "What brings you to our school specifically?"
- "What questions do you have about our program?"

**What to prepare:**

Come in with two or three specific, concrete observations of your child. Not "she loves learning" — but "last week she spent 45 minutes trying to figure out why her LEGO tower kept tipping, and she didn't ask for help once." Specific moments tell evaluators more than adjectives.

Be ready to describe a growth area honestly. This is not a trap. It signals that you see your child clearly and that you'll be a genuine partner when teachers bring you news you didn't expect.

And do the research. Showing up to a parent interview with questions that are answered on the school's homepage signals a lack of engagement with the school specifically — as opposed to private school generally. Schools want families who chose them, not families who are applying everywhere and hoping something sticks.

## The Shy Child, the Wild Child, the Anxious Child

Three temperament types generate most of the parent anxiety heading into playdates. Here is what experienced advisors consistently say about each.

**The shy or slow-to-warm child.** SF schools that use observation-based assessments have been running them for decades. Every evaluator has seen hundreds of children who take time to engage. What evaluators note is the arc — not just the first ten minutes. A child who hangs back initially but is quietly building something at the block table by minute 40 and tentatively joins a conversation by minute 70 is showing evaluators exactly what they're looking for: a child who processes before engaging, and who gets there in their own time. Slow-to-warm is not a liability. A child who never engages at all across 90 minutes is a different picture.

**The very active or impulsive child.** Four-year-olds are physical creatures. Schools that build their programs around four-year-olds know this. What concerns evaluators is not energy or exuberance — it's persistence and intensity at the extreme end. A child who runs around enthusiastically, who is loud, who gets excited and needs a few reminders: this is a four-year-old. A child who is continuously disruptive in ways that prevent any other child from participating, or who becomes physically aggressive: that's different. Progressive SF schools in particular are looking for children who bring energy and curiosity. An active child is often a good match.

**The anxious child.** If your child tends toward anxiety, the most useful thing you can do between now and the playdate is two things: prepare them calmly for what will happen (more on this in Chapter 5), and manage your own anxiety on the day. Children absorb parental stress with remarkable precision. A parent who is visibly nervous at drop-off transmits that signal to the child before the child has set foot in the room. Evaluators see this. They also see parents who hand the child off calmly and clearly: "You're going to play and I'll be right here when you're done." The child's anxiety is the child's. Don't add yours to it.

**Quick reference — how evaluators typically read each temperament type:**

| Temperament | What evaluators usually see | When it becomes a real concern |
|-------------|----------------------------|-------------------------------|
| **Shy / slow-to-warm** | Initial hanging back; gradual engagement; building quietly alone; tentative peer connection later in session | Child shows no engagement at all across the full 90 minutes |
| **Very active / impulsive** | High energy, running, loud enthusiasm; needs a few redirects | Continuous disruption preventing others from participating; physical aggression |
| **Anxious** | Initial distress at drop-off; settling within 10–20 minutes; periodic check-ins with a trusted adult | Inconsolable for majority of session; unable to participate in any activity |

*This table reflects advisor-reported patterns, not published school criteria.*

## Artifact: A Practical Observation Guide

*Use this table before a playdate to assess where your child currently is — not as a checklist to coach against. The temperament matrix above tells you how to interpret what you see; this table tells you what to look for.*

*The following reflects what admissions consultants who work with Bay Area families consistently report evaluators observe during K playdates. No school publishes its internal rubric. This is a guide to where to focus attention — not a scoring sheet, and not school-confirmed policy.*

| Dimension | What it looks like in a 4–5 year old |
|-----------|---------------------------------------|
| **Social-Emotional Readiness** | Takes turns with occasional prompting. Uses words for needs (not just grabbing or crying). Recovers from small frustrations within a few minutes with some support. Begins to engage with unfamiliar adults within the session. |
| **Communication and Language** | Speaks in sentences. Answers "what" and "why" questions. Sustains a back-and-forth exchange with adults and at least attempts conversation with peers. Directs comments or requests to another child, not only to adults. |
| **Curiosity and Engagement** | Approaches new activities with interest. Spends time with one activity rather than cycling constantly between stations. Asks questions about what they see. Makes an attempt before requesting help or giving up. |
| **Self-Regulation and Transitions** | Can shift activities when a teacher signals it's time (may need a reminder, but responds). Waits briefly for a turn without complete breakdown. Participates in a short group activity (10–15 minutes) without continuous disruption. |
| **Independence and Self-Care** | Manages bathroom independently. Handles basic belongings (coat, bag). Opens snack containers. Asks an adult for help when needed rather than becoming distressed and waiting to be noticed. |
| **Problem-Solving and Adaptability** | Tries a different approach when something doesn't work (puzzle, construction, drawing). Recovers from small failures without extended distress. Responds to a new or ambiguous activity with interest rather than immediate shutdown. |

**What this table is not:** a ceiling. A child who demonstrates all of these behaviors consistently and fluently is a four-year-old who is quite socially mature. Most children who get admitted to SF schools show most of these behaviors most of the time — with wobbles, with moments of regression, with the full range of normal four-year-old behavior threaded throughout. Evaluators know the range. They've seen thousands of children. They are not looking for perfection. They are looking for readiness.
