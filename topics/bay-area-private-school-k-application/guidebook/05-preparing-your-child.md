# Chapter 5: Preparing Your Child — Without Over-Preparing

---

The instinct is understandable. You're about to send your four-year-old into a room with strangers to be observed for ninety minutes, and the outcome affects which school they'll attend for the next nine years. Of course you want to prepare them.

The question isn't whether to prepare. It's what preparation actually helps.

This chapter draws a line between two things that look similar from the outside but produce opposite results. On one side: genuine developmental support, the kind of daily engagement that builds the real skills schools observe. On the other side: coaching and drilling, which trains children to perform in ways evaluators recognize immediately as unnatural — and which does nothing for the underlying development that playdates are actually measuring.

Paula Molligan, an admissions consultant frequently cited in Bay Area private school discussions, put it directly: "There isn't one school that we know of that really cares or bothers to check whether a child is reading." Academic advancement is not what's being evaluated. This chapter explains what is.

---

## What Schools Mean by "Readiness"

Chapter 3 described the six dimensions schools observe: social-emotional development, communication and language, curiosity and engagement, self-regulation and transitions, independence and self-care, and problem-solving and adaptability.

These are not academic dimensions. They're developmental dimensions — and they have a specific relationship to age that's worth understanding clearly.

The CDC's developmental milestone framework — built from research on what 75% or more of children can do by a given age — gives us the most reliable anchor for what typical 4–5 year old development looks like. These milestones describe the floor, not the ceiling. They're not what exceptional children do. They're what most children do by a given age.

The CDC's milestones describe what 75% or more of children can do by a given age — a reliable baseline for typical development, not a ceiling. The AAP's HealthyChildren.org lists comparable markers.

| Domain | Typical 4-year-old | Typical 5-year-old | Why it matters in playdates |
|--------|-------------------|--------------------|----------------------------|
| **Language** | Follows multi-step instructions; answers "what is it for" questions | Narrates multi-event stories; maintains extended back-and-forth conversation | Evaluators observe whether children can communicate needs and engage with others |
| **Social-emotional** | Engages in symbolic pretend play; shows concern when others are hurt | Follows rules and takes turns in group games | The core of what observation-based schools observe most |
| **Attention** | — | Pays attention for 5–10 minutes during structured activities | Relevant to group sessions and activity transitions |
| **Fine motor / self-care** | Holds crayon between fingers/thumb (not fist) | Writes some letters in their name; uses utensils | Basic independence in classroom settings |
| **Cognitive** | Names a few colors; basic reasoning about object use | Counts to 10; identifies some letters | May appear in some assessment formats; usually not the primary differentiator |

*Sources: CDC Developmental Milestones (ages 4–5); AAP HealthyChildren.org.*

None of this is about reading novels or doing arithmetic. Private school kindergarten programs don't expect that. What they look for is whether a child is developing on track across the social, emotional, language, and cognitive domains — and whether the pattern of their development suggests they can thrive in a classroom community.

## The Right Kind of Preparation

Everything in this section describes enriched daily life — not test preparation, not coaching. The distinction is important. These activities build genuine developmental capacities over months and years. They're not useful if you start them the week before a playdate.

### Build vocabulary through real conversation

Read together every day. Not to practice reading — to talk. Ask questions about the story. "What do you think will happen next?" "Why do you think the rabbit did that?" "What would you do if you were the character?" These exchanges build the verbal comprehension, narrative following, and back-and-forth conversation skills that language evaluations are actually measuring.

Vocabulary grows through exposure, not drilling. Encountering words in context — in books, in conversation, in the world — is what expands a child's language range. A child who has heard "enormous" used in three different stories uses the word naturally. A child who was given a vocabulary list memorized "enormous" as a definition.

### Let them struggle (briefly) before helping

Self-regulation and problem-solving develop through mild challenge, not through smooth paths. When a puzzle piece doesn't fit, when a building falls down, when a drawing doesn't look the way they envisioned — the parent's instinct is often to help immediately. Resist this slightly. Not for a long time; not to the point of frustration. Just long enough for the child to try a second approach. "What else could you try?" is often more useful than solving the problem for them.

Children who experience this consistently develop the cognitive flexibility and resilience that evaluators describe as "adaptability." They've learned that struggle precedes success, and that they can handle it.

### Give two- and three-step directions in everyday life

"Put your shoes by the door and then wash your hands for dinner." "Get your jacket, close the door, and meet me at the car." These multi-step instructions, woven into ordinary daily routines, build working memory — the ability to hold a sequence in mind and execute it. Schools observe whether children can follow adult directions during the playdate. They're observing the product of months of this kind of practice.

### Make transitions predictable rather than abrupt

Activity transitions — the moment when something fun stops and something new begins — are one of the most reliably difficult moments for four-year-olds. If your child consistently struggles with transitions, you're not alone: this is developmentally normal. But the habit of managed transitions makes assessment day substantially smoother.

Give advance warnings: "We're going to leave the park in five minutes." "Finish what you're building — we're putting away the LEGOs after this part." Let the child have a moment to complete something before the transition happens. Over time, this builds the self-regulation muscle that evaluators are watching during the shift from one activity to the next.

### Arrange peer play, especially with children they don't know well

Private school playdates are full of unfamiliar children. If your child only plays with the same three close friends, the social experience of joining a group of strangers will feel more disorienting than it needs to. Arrange occasional playdates with children from preschool who aren't in your regular social circle. Go to park programs, library storytime, drop-in gym classes. The goal isn't a wide social network — it's comfort with unfamiliar peer contexts.

### Build basic self-care independence

Buttoning a coat. Opening a lunch container. Knowing where the bathroom is and being able to go independently. Using a napkin. These are the skills that appear in the "independence and self-care" dimension — and they're built entirely through the opportunity to do them at home. If your child has always had help with buttons, practice buttons. If they've never tried to open their own snack container, practice snack containers. Simple, unglamorous, entirely within your control.

## The Wrong Kind of Preparation

This section is shorter because the guidance is simpler: don't do it.

**Don't coach your child on answers.** "If they ask you your name, say it clearly." "If they ask you what you like to do, say you like building." Admissions consultants who work with Bay Area families consistently report that rehearsed answers are recognizable to experienced evaluators — a child producing coached responses sounds different from a child speaking naturally. The coached quality doesn't help; it can signal that the parent didn't trust the child to be themselves, which raises its own questions about fit.

**Don't over-index on academic drilling.** Beyond the Brochure, an established admissions consulting publication, is explicit that observation-based playdates are not primarily testing academic skills or performance under pressure. Some assessment formats may still include simple tasks — naming letters, counting objects, identifying colors — but advisors consistently report those are not the central differentiator. A child who has been drilled heavily on letter recognition and counting but who can't manage a transition or engage with a peer is not the profile observation-based schools are primarily selecting for.

**Don't expose IQ-testing children to test-like materials.** If your child is applying to Nueva, Helios, or Harker and will be taking the WPPSI-IV, commercial test prep materials (TestingMom, BrightKids, and similar services that simulate WPPSI content) carry real risks. Nueva and Helios both flag this explicitly in their official materials: prior exposure to test content can affect result validity for at least 12 months, and psychologists are trained to notice signs of preparation. Chapter 4 covers the mechanics of why.

**Don't over-schedule "enrichment" in the weeks before.** A tired, overstimulated child who has been to four activities this week does not perform better on assessment day. The most useful preparation in the days immediately before a playdate is maintaining routine: normal bedtime, normal meals, normal amounts of play. Not novelty. Not intensity.

## What Helps vs. What Backfires — At a Glance

| Helps | Backfires |
|-------|-----------|
| Daily reading and real conversation | Drilling vocabulary lists or academic flashcards |
| Age-appropriate puzzles as part of normal life | Practicing WPPSI-style tasks (for IQ-testing schools) |
| Peer play with unfamiliar children | Over-scheduling enrichment classes before the visit |
| Predictable transitions with advance warnings | Abrupt, high-tension schedule changes |
| Practicing self-care (buttons, snack containers, bathroom) | Doing everything for the child to save time |
| Calm, brief explanation of what will happen | High-stakes framing ("this is really important") |
| Normal sleep and meals leading up to assessment day | Rehearsing answers to likely questions |
| Clean, warm goodbye at drop-off | Prolonged or visibly anxious farewell |

*This reflects patterns from admissions consultants and the Beyond the Brochure publication — not published school policy.*

## Assessment Day — Practical Guidance

This section covers what to actually do in the days and morning before a playdate or assessment.

### The week before

Maintain your child's routine. This is not the week to introduce a new preschool schedule, travel across time zones, or host houseguests who keep children up past their bedtime. Consistency is the only useful "preparation" at this point.

If your child is slow to warm in new environments, and if the school offers a tour option before the assessment date, consider taking one. Walking through the space, seeing some of the rooms, meeting a staff member or two — this familiarization reduces separation anxiety without constituting coaching. Beyond the Brochure specifically recommends this as preparation for slow-to-warm children.

### The morning of

Prioritize sleep and a real breakfast. An overtired, hungry child will not perform at their best, and that's simply true regardless of anything else about admissions.

Tell your child what will happen — briefly, accurately, and without loading it with significance. "Today we're going to visit a school. You'll meet some kids and do some activities. I'll wait for you and we'll do [something they like] afterward." That's enough. Don't explain why it matters. Don't rehearse what to say or do. Don't tell them it's important. They're four. The significance you attach to the morning is something you carry, not them.

Say goodbye cleanly. Prolonged or anxious goodbyes at drop-off communicate distress to the child. "Have fun, I'll be right here when you're done" — and then let the school staff take it from there. Schools are experienced at welcoming children through this transition.

**If your child is having a genuinely bad day when you arrive:** Beyond the Brochure's guidance is direct: "try to gently encourage him/her to separate from you and participate. If he/she refuses, politely ask the teacher to help. If that doesn't work, as a last resort, ask to come back another day. This happens occasionally and schools expect kids to have bad days." Rescheduling is possible. Ask politely, and most schools accommodate this.

### Manage your own visible anxiety

This bears repeating because it is the most controllable variable you have. Children read parental emotional states with remarkable precision. A parent who is visibly tense, who hovers, who gives the child one last set of instructions at the door — that parent is transmitting a message: *this is dangerous, be careful*.

Multiple sources — child development research and admissions advisor accounts alike — point to the same pattern: parent anxiety at drop-off is one of the most significant external factors in how children approach unfamiliar situations. You cannot control what your child does in the room. You can control what emotional signal you transmit at the door. Calm, warm, matter-of-fact: that's the target.

## What's Actually Being Assessed — And What Isn't

Let's name the things clearly, because the private school admissions culture can blur them.

The following reflects what admissions consultants who work with Bay Area families — and maintain ongoing contact with school admissions offices — consistently report. Schools don't publish their observation criteria; this is synthesized from advisor accounts, not confirmed by schools directly.

**What advisors say evaluators are watching for:**

- Whether the child can engage with unfamiliar adults and peers
- Whether the child can manage transitions with a prompt or two
- Whether the child shows genuine curiosity and willingness to try new things
- Whether the child's communication is functional for a classroom context
- Whether the child can sustain attention during a structured group activity
- Whether the child can recover from small frustrations without prolonged distress

**What advisors say observation-based playdates are usually not primarily optimizing for:**

- Advanced academic performance — reading early, counting past 100, reciting the full alphabet on command
- Polished performance of specific skills in front of strangers
- Extroversion or talkativeness

Some assessment formats may still include simple tasks — naming letters, counting a small set of objects, identifying colors — but advisors consistently report that these are not the central differentiator. The evaluation covers far more than basic academic readiness.

Admissions consultant Paula Molligan, quoted in Ruth Krishnan's widely-cited San Francisco admissions guide, put it directly: "There isn't one school that we know of that really cares or bothers to check whether a child is reading." That's a practitioner who works directly with schools making an explicit statement about advanced reading specifically — not a claim that academic tasks never appear in any assessment format.

## A Realistic Standard

The question parents should be asking is not "Is my child ahead?" It's "Is my child developing on track across these dimensions, and does their development look like what this particular school environment is designed to support?"

A child who takes five minutes to warm up before they start engaging — that's fine. A child who has one meltdown during the snack transition and then recovers and finishes the activity — that's fine. A child who doesn't speak much at first but then starts building alongside another child without needing adult mediation — that's fine.

Most children who are developing typically and who have had normal exposure to peer play settings will show what evaluators are looking for. Not perfectly, not without moments of four-year-old behavior — but adequately, honestly, genuinely. The CDC milestones describe what that looks like. That's the bar.

## Your Week-Before Checklist

| Day | Action |
|-----|--------|
| 7 days before | Confirm logistics: time, location, what to bring |
| 5 days before | Check sleep routine — move bedtime earlier if child has been staying up late |
| 3 days before | Arrange a "normal" playdate (peers, not parents) so child gets peer interaction |
| 1 day before | Consider a campus visit or drive-by if child is slow to warm |
| Morning of | Prioritize a real breakfast and enough time to avoid rushing |
| Drop-off | Brief, warm, matter-of-fact goodbye — "Have fun, I'll be right here" |
| Afterward | Do something your child enjoys; don't debrief the experience at length |

The debrief is worth flagging specifically: don't ask your child to tell you everything that happened. Don't pump them for details to assess how they performed. A casual "Did you have fun?" and then moving on to the rest of the day is the right call. Your child experienced an activity. They don't need to narrate it back to you for your evaluation. They need lunch.
