# Chapter 1: Is Private K Right for You?

---

This book assumes you are seriously considering private kindergarten. But most families who reach the application process haven't yet answered the question that this chapter is about.

That question is not: which private school?

It's: why am I considering private school — and is that reason real?

This matters because there are good reasons to apply to private K and weak reasons to apply to private K. Families who know which they're acting on make better decisions — both about whether to apply and about what they're looking for when they do. Families who haven't done this examination often end up in a process that doesn't serve them.

---

## What You Think You're Deciding

Most families approaching private K feel like they're making a school choice. They visit campuses, compare programs, read parent forum threads. It feels like a consumer research process with an educational product at the end.

That frame is incomplete.

What you're actually doing is making a commitment decision. The application process is about finding a kindergarten. What you're choosing is whether to make a financial commitment that, in many Bay Area families' cases, will span nine to thirteen years and total more money than many mortgages.

Bay Area education nonprofits that model K-12 private school costs estimate the total at $500,000–$700,000 per child — based on current tuition levels with assumed annual increases of 4–6%. These are modeled projections, not reported statistics, and they carry uncertainty in both directions. But the order of magnitude is not disputed: if you're enrolling a child in private K today, the honest conversation is about what happens in years two, five, and nine — not just year one.

Most families don't have that conversation before the application process. They look at the kindergarten tuition, calculate whether it's manageable for one year, and proceed. The ones who regret it later often say the same thing: they didn't model the full commitment.

If you have more than one child who would attend private school, multiply the models by the number of children who would follow. Private school is an individual commitment that scales.

## The Three Real Reasons Families Choose Private K

Families who have thought carefully about why they're applying — not just reacted to anxiety — tend to cite a specific set of reasons that actually hold up to scrutiny. There are three.

### 1. A specific program your public school doesn't offer

Gifted programs (Nueva, Helios, Harker), language immersion (CAIS for Mandarin, La Scuola for Italian, Lycee for French), specific pedagogical approaches (Montessori, Waldorf, progressive child-led learning) that SFUSD doesn't offer in your assignment zone. These are real.

The test for whether this is your reason: can you name the program? Can you name the school that has it? If the answer is "a better education" rather than "a Spanish immersion program that my child won't get through the SFUSD lottery," that is not yet a specific reason — it's a general aspiration.

Specific reasons lead to specific schools. Specific reasons also make the cost feel proportionate to what you're actually getting, because you know what you're actually getting.

### 2. Certainty that your public school lottery can't provide

San Francisco families face a dynamic that most families in other cities don't: SFUSD does not assign students to neighborhood schools by default. Families submit a ranked preference list, and a lottery algorithm determines placement. Results arrive in March — roughly the same time private school decisions come.

According to Basic Fund, a Bay Area education nonprofit, lottery uncertainty is one of the primary drivers that pushes SF families toward private school applications — not necessarily because they prefer private school, but because they want certainty. If the lottery gives you your first- or second-choice school, you have a real alternative. If your realistic public assignment is a school far from your neighborhood whose culture feels like a poor fit, the private school application carries more weight.

For families outside San Francisco, this factor largely disappears. The calculation is different when your neighborhood school is your assignment.

### 3. A specific community whose culture genuinely matches your family's

Some Bay Area private schools have cultures that meaningfully differ from what public schools offer — smaller, more intimate parent communities, more explicit focus on particular values, stronger integration between home and school life. This is real, and it is a legitimate reason.

The honest version of this reason is more demanding than it sounds, though. The families who report the strongest satisfaction with private K are usually ones who have visited enough schools to distinguish between the community they actually want and the community they think looks good. That distinction matters: a community that looks like you isn't the same as a community that thinks like you. The first is easier to find than the second.

## The Three Reasons That Sound Right But Aren't

These appear in parent forums, in conversations with advisors, and in parents' internal monologue. They are worth examining honestly.

### "Better outcomes"

Private school outcomes research is messier than private school marketing suggests. At the K level particularly, evidence for meaningfully different academic outcomes from private versus strong public schooling is limited. The families whose children thrive at great private schools and the families whose children thrive at great public schools are often more similar than the schools would like you to think.

This doesn't mean private school produces no benefit — it means "better outcomes" is not a defensible reason on its own, because the data doesn't clearly support it and because the version of the benefit that does exist is usually program-specific, not school-sector-specific.

### "Better teachers"

Teacher quality varies at private and public schools. Experienced observers of Bay Area education generally note that both sectors have strong teachers and weak ones. Private school teachers may have more autonomy and smaller classes, but "better" is not a reliable generalization. If you have a specific reason to believe a specific school's faculty is unusually strong in a dimension that matters for your child, that's a specific claim you can test. The general claim is harder to verify.

### "The network"

At the K level, "the network" is almost entirely a story parents tell themselves about what will happen in year twelve — not a real current benefit. For young children, the primary benefit of school is the school itself: the teaching, the peer community, the environment your child spends their days in. Projecting an adult professional network onto a kindergarten decision is reaching past the evidence in a way that tends to make the financial calculation feel more justified than it is.

## The SFUSD Factor: A Specific Context for SF Families

If you're in San Francisco, the public school lottery is worth understanding clearly.

You can apply to both public and private school simultaneously without foreclosing either option. The private school application serves, in part, as insurance against a disappointing lottery placement. And the lottery placement you might receive matters enormously — a strong SFUSD first-choice school and a disappointing private school outcome may genuinely leave you wondering whether you needed to do any of this.

Before deciding how seriously to pursue private K, know your realistic public alternatives. Research the schools you're actually likely to receive through the lottery — not the best possible outcome, the realistic one. Talk to families currently at those schools. Some are genuinely strong programs. Some are genuinely not a fit for particular children. Know which you're facing before you've spent six months and several thousand dollars on an application process.

## Three Paths Worth Considering Fully

### The full K-12 commitment

This is the standard model, and it's the model this book is primarily written for: a family that's made a genuine long-term decision and wants to navigate the process well. If you've looked at the costs, modeled the years out, and decided, the rest of this book will help you do it effectively.

### Parochial schools: a real middle path many families overlook

Bay Area parochial and religious private schools — Catholic schools, Jewish day schools, and others — operate at a substantially different price point from independent schools. Current parochial tuition runs approximately $6,000–$10,000 per year, compared to $40,000–$55,000 at independent schools. That's roughly 75–85% less expensive while still offering many of the structural features families seek: smaller classes, a committed community, stable enrollment, and certainty (no lottery).

The trade-offs are genuine: parochial schools have a religious dimension that doesn't fit every family. The pedagogy is typically more traditional and less likely to be the progressive, child-led approaches that many Bay Area families are drawn to.

But for families who want the environment of private school and face real budget constraints, parochial school is a path worth evaluating on its merits — not as a consolation prize.

### Private as a bridge strategy

Some Bay Area families pursue a middle path: enroll in private K with explicit intention to reassess annually, not as a full K-12 commitment but as a one-year commitment renewed if the school continues to be the right fit.

This works because you are never locked out of public school. SFUSD accepts mid-year transfers and new enrollments in subsequent years. Some families find that one or two years of private school provides a specific developmental foundation — language immersion, a particular early learning environment — and then transition to public school for later grades.

The honest accounting on this strategy: you pay one or more years of private school tuition at the same per-year cost, without the long-term investment framing that families use to justify it. It is a real option, but it requires genuine annual re-evaluation. The specific risk is inertia: many families who planned to "reassess" find that the school community, the routine, and their child's attachment make leaving harder than they anticipated. If you're considering this path, think through not just "will I reassess" but "what would make me actually leave."

## The Financial Question, Honestly

Chapter 8 walks through the financial aid calculation and the true cost worksheet in detail. Before you get there, two things worth knowing now.

First: the income thresholds at which Bay Area private schools provide meaningful financial aid are higher than most families expect. Nueva School, the only Bay Area school that publishes specific income brackets, provides aid to families earning above $250,000. Run the aid calculation before assuming you don't qualify. Assuming is not a substitute for applying.

Second: the financial question is not "can we afford year one." It's "can we afford years one through nine — including the 4–6% annual increases — without meaningful financial strain." A school you can sustain without strain, including in a harder year, is a better choice than one that requires everything to go right.

Families whose children have documented learning differences requiring IEP or 504 services are worth calling out specifically. Under federal law, public schools are legally required to provide these services; private schools are not. Some Bay Area private schools have robust learning support programs; others have very little. Evaluating a private school's support model requires specific questions about staffing and capacity, not general reassurances. And the public school's legal obligation is a meaningful baseline that's worth weighing explicitly against whatever a private school offers.

## Making the Decision

There isn't a right answer, and this book isn't going to tell you that private K is obviously the correct choice for your family. It's the right choice for many families and the wrong one for others, and the difference is specific to your child, your finances, your public school alternatives, and your values.

What makes the decision clearer:

**Name your real reason.** Not "a better education" — something specific enough that you could explain it to a skeptic. If you can't yet name it clearly, you're not yet making a real decision.

**Know your public school alternative.** Research your realistic SFUSD assignment options before concluding they're not adequate. The public option you're actually likely to receive — not the best possible one — is the comparison that matters.

**Model the full commitment, not year one.** Chapter 8 has the worksheet. Do it before you apply.

## Artifact: Your Private School Decision Checklist

Before starting applications, work through this:

| Question | Your answer |
|----------|-------------|
| What specifically is my realistic public school assignment? | |
| Would my child thrive there? (honest answer, not hoped-for answer) | |
| Can I name a specific program or approach at a private school that my public option doesn't offer? What is it? | |
| Is my primary motivation one of the three real reasons — specific program, SFUSD uncertainty, community fit? Or something else? | |
| What is the realistic annual tuition at the schools I'm considering? | |
| What is my realistic aid estimate? (Chapter 8 can help) | |
| Can I sustain tuition × ~9 years, with 4–6% annual increases, without significant strain — including in a harder year? | |
| If I have more than one child who would attend, have I modeled the full cost for all of them? | |
| Have I seriously considered parochial schools if budget is a constraint? | |
| If I'm planning a bridge strategy, what concrete conditions would make me actually leave? | |

There's no score. But families who can answer these questions clearly — even when the answers are uncomfortable — make better decisions than families who proceed without asking them.

If your honest answers point toward private school, the rest of this book will help you do it well. If they point somewhere else, that's real information too, and it's better to have it now than after six months of applications.
