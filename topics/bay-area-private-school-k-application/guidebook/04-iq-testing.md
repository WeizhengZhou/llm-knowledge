# Chapter 4: IQ Testing — Demystified

---

Most families applying to private kindergarten in the Bay Area will never deal with IQ testing. If your list includes SF Day School, Hamlin, SF Friends, or any of the major San Francisco schools, this chapter doesn't apply to your admissions process at all. You can skip to Chapter 5.

But if your list includes Nueva School, Helios School, or Harker School — or if you're considering any of them — you're in a different situation. According to each school's official admissions materials, an IQ assessment administered by a licensed psychologist is part of a complete application. This chapter answers the questions that come with that requirement: what the test measures, what the appointment is like, how to find a psychologist, and what to do with the results.

## What the WPPSI-IV Actually Measures

The WPPSI-IV — Wechsler Preschool and Primary Scale of Intelligence, Fourth Edition — is the standard cognitive assessment for children ages 2 years, 6 months through 7 years, 7 months. It's published by Pearson and administered by licensed psychologists. It is not a single "IQ score" from a single test — it's a battery of subtests organized into five cognitive indices, each measuring something distinct.

Here is what each index actually measures, in terms that describe what you see in a child's daily life:

**Verbal Comprehension Index (VCI)** measures a child's ability to understand and use language — vocabulary, verbal reasoning, and the capacity to express concepts in words. A child with high VCI tends to have a large vocabulary for their age, asks "why" questions with follow-up, and can explain their reasoning in conversation. A child with emerging VCI might struggle to find the right word for an idea they clearly have, or give one-word answers to open-ended questions.

**Visual Spatial Index (VSI)** measures the ability to understand spatial relationships, recognize patterns, and mentally rotate or manipulate shapes and structures. Children with high VSI often excel at building, puzzles, and spatial reasoning tasks — they see how pieces fit together. This index underlies skills like reading a map, understanding geometry, or building intricate block structures.

**Fluid Reasoning Index (FRI)** measures the ability to identify rules and patterns, apply logic to novel problems, and think flexibly. It's sometimes described as "on-the-spot" thinking — what a child does when encountering a problem they've never seen before. High FRI tends to show up as children who figure things out without being told, who notice patterns in unfamiliar situations, and who try different approaches when one solution doesn't work.

**Working Memory Index (WMI)** measures the ability to hold information in mind and use it — similar to mental "scratch pad" capacity. In daily life, this is what allows a child to follow a three-step direction, remember what they were going to say while listening to someone else, or keep track of rules during a game. Emerging working memory often looks like forgetting instructions mid-task or losing track in multi-step activities.

**Processing Speed Index (PSI)** measures the speed and accuracy with which a child processes simple information. It's the efficiency of cognitive work — how quickly can the child complete a straightforward visual task correctly? Higher PSI means working quickly and accurately on routine tasks; lower PSI doesn't indicate lower overall intelligence, but may mean the child needs more time to demonstrate what they know.

### FSIQ vs. GAI — What's the Difference?

The **Full Scale IQ (FSIQ)** is a composite score derived from all five indices. It's the number most commonly referenced — including in Nueva's published threshold of approximately 130.

The **General Ability Index (GAI)** is a partial composite using only the Verbal Comprehension, Visual Spatial, and Fluid Reasoning indices, excluding Working Memory and Processing Speed. The GAI is considered useful when a child has a significant gap between their intellectual ability and their processing efficiency — for example, a highly verbal and conceptually strong child who processes routine tasks more slowly. Psychologists and some schools use GAI as a supplemental lens when FSIQ may underrepresent a child's cognitive profile.

Helios specifically requests both scores in the psychologist's report. If you're applying to schools that require IQ results, confirm with your psychologist that the report includes both FSIQ and GAI.

## What the Appointment Is Like

For most children, the IQ testing appointment doesn't feel like a test. That's by design.

The assessment is conducted one-on-one with a licensed psychologist in a private office. Before beginning formal tasks, the psychologist typically spends time building rapport with the child — talking casually, establishing comfort. For young children, the transition into actual test tasks happens gradually and naturally. The tasks themselves are designed to feel game-like: arranging blocks, identifying patterns on picture cards, pointing to images in sequence. There are no pencils and bubble sheets.

According to the SF Standard's reporting on Bay Area IQ testing, most psychologists describe the experience as one that young children don't register as a "test" at all.

Duration varies:
- **WPPSI-IV for K-age children (approximately 45–90 minutes):** The standard assessment for most 4–5 year old applicants
- **Harker's full battery (90 minutes to 2 hours):** Harker requires all applicable subtests with no abbreviations, which extends the session

One parent's experience, as reported in the SF Standard: "I felt nervous, as I wasn't sure if she would get upset leaving me." Her daughter was fine. The session went normally. Parents consistently report that anticipatory anxiety about the appointment is worse than the appointment itself — for the child, if not for the parent.

**Where are you during the test?** In the waiting area. Parents do not accompany children into the testing room. This is standard practice across all providers and schools — the assessment measures independent cognitive performance.

## Score Cutoffs — And What Happens If You Miss

**Nueva** is the only school with a publicly stated threshold: FSIQ around 130 (98th percentile), per the school's official admissions page. If results fall below this range, Nueva withdraws the application. The child is not eligible to retest for 12 months.

**Helios** has no published FSIQ minimum. The school reviews the whole report and considers relative strengths across indices. Families apply without knowing a specific threshold to hit.

**Harker** has no published FSIQ minimum and uses results holistically alongside interviews, recommendations, and academic records.

Dr. Rachel Holowicki, a Bay Area educational psychologist quoted in the SF Standard, noted that "an IQ test is a snapshot in time" — and that she reminds parents of this regularly. Scores reflect current ability and current testing conditions, not a fixed ceiling. A 4-year-old's score may differ significantly from scores taken years later.

Frank Worrell, professor at UC Berkeley, noted in the same article that giftedness correlates with socioeconomic status and opportunity access — factors that are themselves products of circumstance, not innate capacity. The score from one morning's appointment is real information, but it is not a final verdict.

## Retesting — The 12-Month Rule

**What Nueva and Helios state explicitly:** if a child has been exposed to test content before their assessment — whether through commercial practice materials, coaching, or a prior test that was invalidated — they cannot retest for at least 12 months from the prior test date. This is stated in both schools' official materials.

**What is general psychometric practice:** the 12-month waiting period is also standard in professional testing ethics because the "practice effect" is real — a child who has seen test content before cannot reliably produce valid results until the content is no longer fresh. Psychologists are trained to flag signs of prior exposure.

What this means in practice:
- **Do not use commercial test prep materials** (TestingMom, BrightKids, or similar services that simulate WPPSI content). Both Nueva and Helios explicitly flag these in their materials, and general psychometric standards hold that prior exposure invalidates results.
- **If your child has already been tested** for another purpose (developmental evaluation, learning difference assessment), inform your psychologist. Prior results don't automatically prevent new testing, but your psychologist needs the full picture to assess timing appropriately.
- **If your child's results fall below Nueva's threshold**, the 12-month wait applies before retesting — meaning the earliest realistic opportunity is the following admissions cycle.

## What to Tell Your Child — and What Not to Do

Schools and psychologists recommend telling children the appointment is "a playdate with a doctor who will do puzzles and ask questions." This framing is accurate and age-appropriate — it neither understates the session (so the child isn't surprised) nor loads it with significance the child can't yet process.

> **Appropriate preparation vs. what can invalidate results**
>
> | Appropriate | Can invalidate results |
> |-------------|----------------------|
> | Regular sleep and normal meals leading up to the appointment | Commercial test prep materials (TestingMom, BrightKids, similar) |
> | Daily reading and conversation | Practicing WPPSI-style tasks or patterns from any source |
> | Age-appropriate puzzles and games as part of normal daily life | Coaching your child on specific question types or answer strategies |
> | Calm, brief explanation of what will happen | Rehearsing specific questions or answers with the child |
> | Brief visit to the assessment location if child is anxious about new environments | Exposing the child to test-like materials within 12 months of the appointment |
>
> *The invalidation risk for commercial prep materials is stated explicitly in Nueva's and Helios's official materials. General psychometric standards hold that prior exposure to test content affects validity for at least 12 months.*

**One thing to manage on the day:** your own visible anxiety during drop-off. Children are attentive to parental emotional signals. A parent who appears tense communicates that the situation is high-stakes — which the child then carries into the room. The session will go more smoothly if you're calm at the handoff.

## The Schools That Don't Require IQ Testing

It's worth naming this explicitly because the IQ testing conversation in Bay Area admissions circles can create a distorted impression that all competitive private schools require it.

They don't.

The vast majority of Bay Area private elementary schools — Hamlin, SF Friends School, SF Day School, The San Francisco School, Live Oak, Presidio Hill, Head-Royce, Park Day, Marin Country Day, Convent & Stuart Hall, Children's Day School, and many others — use observation-based playdates and parent interviews. No IQ test. No score cutoff. No psychologist.

If your list doesn't include Nueva, Helios, or Harker, nothing in this chapter applies to your process. Chapter 3 — the six dimensions of observation-based assessment — is the chapter that covers what those schools are actually evaluating.

## Your Three-Step Plan

If your list includes any IQ-testing school:

**Step 1 (May–August):** Research psychologists. Look at Nueva's resource list if Nueva is on your list. Contact 2–3 providers to understand their availability and booking process. Don't book yet — just know who you'll call.

**Step 2 (Early September):** Book the appointment. For Nueva's November 3 guarantee deadline, this means September. For January deadlines, October works, but don't push past mid-October.

**Step 3 (After the test):** Get the written report and confirm it includes both FSIQ and GAI scores. Do not discuss scores publicly or with other applicant families. If results come back below Nueva's threshold, have a direct conversation with your psychologist about what the profile shows and what next-cycle options look like.

---

## A Final Note

The IQ testing requirement affects a small subset of Bay Area private school applicants. It exists because Nueva, Helios, and Harker are genuinely gifted-education programs — schools built around a particular cognitive profile that their pedagogy is designed to serve. The assessment is how they identify that profile.

If your child's scores are in range: continue the process. If they aren't: that's also real information — about this school, at this moment, for this child. A score that doesn't open one door is not a verdict on a child's future, or on their intelligence, or on what kind of school community they'll eventually thrive in. Many Bay Area observation-based schools — where most of the best K programs are — don't ask for a number at all.
