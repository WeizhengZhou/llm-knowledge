# Chapter 9: School Tours — Reading Past the Performance

---

Open houses are designed to impress you. They almost always succeed. The question is whether you can read what's real past what's staged — because the school that looks best at an open house and the school that's the best fit for your child are not always the same.

By the time most families reach their third or fourth open house, a familiar frustration sets in. Every school has a beautiful building. Every admissions director speaks with genuine warmth. The parent panels radiate enthusiasm. The classrooms look like something from an architectural magazine. You leave each event thinking, "That was great," and then sit in the car unable to explain what distinguished this school from the last one.

That feeling is the problem this chapter is designed to solve. Not by giving you a longer checklist, but by helping you change what you're looking for.

---

## What Open Houses Are Actually Designed to Show You

Understanding this isn't cynicism. It's the correct frame for everything that follows.

An open house is a curated product. The admissions team has spent weeks planning it. The parents invited to speak are the school's strongest advocates. The classrooms you walk through are at their most camera-ready. The teachers who interact with visiting families are people who are good at this particular interaction. Even the timing of the tour — almost always during school hours, when children are engaged and the school is at its most alive — is a deliberate choice.

None of this is dishonest. Schools are competing for families, and showing their best version is appropriate. But it means that what you observe at an open house is the school's intentions and aspirations at full volume, filtered through its most enthusiastic presenters. What you're trying to hear, underneath all of that, is the everyday reality — what Tuesday morning at 9:30 looks like when there are no visitors and three kids are fighting over the same marker.

The signals that reveal that everyday reality are specific and observable. The ones that reveal only the school's marketing are also specific, and worth knowing.

---

## Six Signals Worth Reading

The following reflects what advisors who work with Bay Area families and observe many school environments report as the most reliable indicators of genuine school culture during open house visits. These are not official evaluation criteria — no school publishes what you should look for when visiting them. They are the observations that experienced practitioners say consistently distinguish schools that walk the walk from schools that talk the talk.

### 1. How Teachers Speak to Children

Advisors who work with Bay Area families consistently name this as one of the highest-signal observations during a school visit — and one that most families walk past without noticing.

Watch not just the warmth but the specificity. Does the teacher address children by name when they arrive? ("Good morning, Marcus — I saw you working on that bridge yesterday. How did it turn out?") Or does the teacher address them as a group, or as a type? ("Good morning, everyone.") The difference sounds small. It isn't.

Pay attention to what happens when a child makes a mistake or struggles while you're observing. A child who knocks over the paint, or who can't figure out the puzzle, or who says "I don't know" to a question — how the teacher responds in that moment tells you something the tour script cannot. A teacher who moves toward the struggle, who makes it unhurried, who says "let's figure it out together" rather than supplying the answer, is demonstrating something about the school's actual culture. A teacher who looks briefly pained and moves on is also demonstrating something.

The overall energy in a classroom is worth noticing — though it's one of the harder signals to read accurately on a single visit. Advisors report that teachers who seem genuinely energized by the work, who are curious about what children are doing rather than just managing them through the day, tend to signal something about school culture that matters for staff retention and morale. One visit is not a verdict; it's a data point to hold alongside others.

### 2. What Children Are Doing to Each Other

In most SF open houses, you will see children at some point — in classrooms being toured, during a demo activity, moving through hallways. Watch the peer dynamics for a few minutes longer than feels comfortable.

Advisors working with Bay Area families note that the peer culture in a school is harder to fake during an open house than almost anything else. Are children making space for each other, or edging each other out? When a child says something odd or awkward, do peers laugh at them or with them or not at all? Is there a social hierarchy that's already visible in a group of kindergarteners, with obvious insiders and outsiders? Or is there a rougher, more generous quality to the interactions — children being naturally, imperfectly inclusive?

One counterintuitive signal worth knowing: schools where children seem intensely well-behaved and orderly during tours are worth examining more carefully, not less. Perfect behavior during a school tour can indicate a culture where children have learned that adults are watching and the correct response is performance. That's very different from a culture where children behave well because they want to, or because the environment is organized in ways that make it natural.

### 3. What's on the Walls

Walk slowly through classrooms with this question: does this work look like it came from the same child, or from twenty different children?

Cookie-cutter wall displays — every child's butterfly colored with the same palette, every child's autumn tree built from the same template — tell you about teacher comfort with predictability. They tell you what the teacher needed the wall to look like. Student work that shows individual voice — where one child's self-portrait is a chaotic riot of color and another child's is nearly photographic and a third child's is mostly a scribble that required negotiation to be hung — tells you that the teachers value process over product and individual expression over uniformity.

Advisors note that this particular signal is easiest to spot and requires no expertise to read. You don't need to know anything about educational philosophy to feel the difference between a hallway that looks like it belongs on Pinterest and a hallway that looks like it belongs to actual children.

Classroom organization also reveals something about pedagogy. A classroom with clear, labeled areas for different types of play and work — organized chaos, rather than rigid military order or genuine mess — signals a teacher who has thought carefully about how children move through space and choose activities. This is more visible at progressive schools, where the physical environment is intentionally set up to support child-led exploration.

### 4. How the Parent Community Talks About Other Schools

This one is subtle, and some families miss it entirely.

At every open house, you will be surrounded by other families who are also visiting, also evaluating, also in the middle of applying to three or four or six schools. Listen to how they talk about the other schools they've visited.

Advisors who work with Bay Area families observe that the parent community you join matters as much as the school itself — you will spend the next eight years at events with these people, and your children will spend those years with their children. A parent community that is openly competitive, that ranks schools publicly, that is visibly anxious and status-conscious in the lobby of an open house, is giving you information about what PTA meetings will feel like. A parent community that seems curious, that asks genuine questions, that appears to be there because they actually want to understand the school — that's different.

What you notice about who is in the room — the range of backgrounds, parenting styles, economic presentations — is also worth registering. It won't be perfect information: open houses self-select. But it's a signal.

### 5. What Admissions Staff Say When You Ask Hard Questions

Most open house questions and answers are thoroughly rehearsed. "What makes your school unique?" produces a version of the school's mission statement. "What's the school's approach to reading instruction?" produces the approved pedagogical answer.

The questions that get real answers are the ones admissions staff haven't practiced deflecting:

- **"What does a child look like who is NOT a good fit here?"** A school that can answer this specifically — not vaguely, not defensively, but with genuine self-awareness about what kind of child or family would be better served elsewhere — is a school that knows itself. Evasion or a generic "we really think all children thrive here" tells you something too.

- **"What's the hardest part of being a parent in this community?"** This question surprises people. Admissions directors who have been through enough cycles will give you a real answer: the annual fund expectations, the intensity of the parent culture, the school's communication challenges, whatever it is. An admissions director who has no answer — or who pivots immediately to talking about what's wonderful — is not ready to be candid with you.

- **"How has the school changed in the last five years?"** Schools that have genuinely evolved can describe it specifically. A new leadership team, a change in the K curriculum, the way they handled COVID and what it taught them. Schools where the honest answer is "not much" often say "not much" — which is itself information.

These questions are not gotcha questions. They're the kind of question a school should be able to answer honestly, and admissions staff who answer them honestly are telling you something important about the institution's relationship with candor.

### 6. Your Own Gut Response — and What It Might Actually Be Tracking

Advisors who work with Bay Area families consistently note that parents' instinctive responses to open house visits are real data — but they come with an important caution.

The gut feeling that says "I loved this place" is worth examining. Is it responding to something specific — the quality of teacher-student interactions you observed, the way the admissions director answered a question, the peer culture you witnessed? Or is it responding to something less reliable — a beautifully renovated facility, a brand you already recognized, a community of parents who look and sound like you?

The school that feels most comfortable is not always the best fit. Familiarity and comfort are easy to mistake for belonging. The school where the teachers spoke to children the way you want your child spoken to, where the work on the walls showed actual children rather than a template, where the admissions director told you something honest — that school may or may not be the one you felt most comfortable in.

Children pick up more than parents expect during school visits. If you bring your child to a prospective family event, what they tell you afterward — not "did you like it?" but "tell me about it" — is worth registering as a data point.

## What to Mostly Ignore

> **The fancy new building.** A recently renovated facility is a nice amenity, and occasionally a signal that the school is financially healthy. It is not a signal of educational quality. Some of the best K programs in the Bay Area operate in buildings that are old, slightly cramped, and unapologetically unglamorous. Some programs operating in stunning new facilities are mediocre. The building is not the school.

> **The impressive list of alumni families.** Every school in this market has alumni kids who went on to great outcomes. Every single one. The school that mentions its alumni most prominently during the open house presentation has made a calculation about what impresses you. Don't let it.

> **The glossy handbook.** The parent handbook is a document. What the school actually does is what you observed on the tour. These are sometimes very similar. They are not always the same.

> **The enthusiasm of the admissions presentation itself.** Admissions directors are chosen partly for their ability to present the school compellingly. A charismatic presentation is evidence of a good presenter. It is not evidence of a great school.

## How to Use Multiple Visits

Advisors who work with Bay Area families recommend that visiting families plan their school tours with a few practical principles in mind.

**Take notes within an hour of leaving — not during.** Being present and observant during the visit is more valuable than documentation. Your notes during the visit will tend toward what was said rather than what you observed. Notes taken immediately after, while impressions are fresh, will tend toward what you felt and noticed — which is more useful. By the end of the day, the visits blur. Write notes before you sleep.

**Visit more schools than you plan to apply to.** Three or four open house visits reveal patterns. One school where teachers all seem tired might be an off-day. Two schools where that's true, and two others where teachers seem genuinely engaged, is a pattern. You need the comparison.

**Distinguish between what you noticed and what you inferred.** "The K teacher called three children by name" is a notice. "This school really values each child as an individual" is an inference. The inference may be correct, but keep the underlying observation visible in your notes — that's what you can compare across schools.

**One practical structure:** rate each school on a 1–5 scale across the six dimensions below, immediately after each visit. Don't show your ratings to your partner before they complete theirs. Then compare. Discrepancy is information. Agreement is confirmation. The exercise forces specificity in a context where impressions tend toward the general.

## Progressive vs. Traditional: Reading the Room

If you've done the research in Chapter 3, you already know that Bay Area private K schools organize themselves around fundamentally different approaches to education. Open house visits are where those differences become visible — and where families who haven't thought clearly about this tend to make costly mistakes.

**What progressive school culture looks like in practice during a tour:**

Advisors who observe many Bay Area school environments describe the typical progressive K classroom as organized around distinct activity areas — blocks, art, dramatic play, literacy, sensory materials — rather than rows of desks or tables arranged for teacher-directed instruction. There is usually more visible choice: children are selecting what they work on, in what order, and sometimes with whom. Teachers spend more time observing and facilitating than directing. The noise level tends to be higher, the organized chaos more visible.

The philosophy here is that children construct understanding through exploration, and that the role of the teacher is to create conditions for that exploration and then get out of the way enough to let it happen. Schools like SF Day School, SF Friends School, Hamlin, Live Oak, and Presidio Hill operate in varying degrees along this spectrum.

**What traditional or structured school culture looks like in practice during a tour:**

A more structured K classroom typically involves more teacher-directed activity, more explicit instruction in academic content, and more visible transition management. Children are more often doing the same thing at the same time. The teacher is more often at the front, or moving through the room to guide individual children through a shared task.

Schools like Challenger and Stratford in the South Bay, and the more structured end of the Peninsula/East Bay market, are more likely to look like this. The philosophy here is that children learn best through clear instruction, structured practice, and explicit feedback — and that kindergarten is not too early to begin building academic habits.

**The fit question is not "which is better."** This is not a debate with a winner. Experienced advisors consistently note that fit is about alignment between a school's approach and your family's rhythms, expectations, and values — not about which philosophy produces better outcomes at the population level.

A family that values clear structure at home, explicit expectations, and academic accountability may find the flow of a progressive classroom confusing or frustrating — for the parent as much as the child. A family that values flexibility, child-led choice, and process over product may find a highly structured classroom rigid and joyless.

Neither of these is a character failing. They're preferences, and they matter for whether your child will thrive — and whether you'll feel like the school is a partner rather than a puzzle.

The practical test during the tour: does the culture you're observing match the culture you're operating in at home? Not in every detail, but in its basic assumptions about how children learn?

| In a progressive classroom, you typically see... | In a structured classroom, you typically see... |
|--------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------|
| Multiple activity stations running simultaneously | More shared, teacher-directed activities |
| Children choosing what to work on and for how long | Common tasks with clear expectations |
| Teachers observing and asking questions more than instructing | Teachers explaining, modeling, and guiding |
| Work that varies by child — different materials, different pace | Work that progresses through the same sequence |
| Transitions facilitated through song, signal, or discussion | Transitions that are brisk and explicit |
| The classroom set up as a resource environment for exploration | The classroom set up for instruction and practice |

*Note: This table reflects general patterns reported by advisors observing Bay Area schools. Individual schools and individual classrooms vary. Use it as a rough orientation, not a definitive sorting system.*
