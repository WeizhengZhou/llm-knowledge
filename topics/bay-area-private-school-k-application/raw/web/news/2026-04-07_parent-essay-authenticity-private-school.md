# Parent Statement Tips for Private School Applications

## Sources

Primary: admission.org (Jamie Moffett, 11 tips)
Secondary: cardinaleducation.com, parentspress.com, prepmatters.com, privateschoolreview.com, evolvededucationcompany.com

## Key Principles

Admissions officers consistently emphasize that **authenticity is the key to effective parent essays**. Admissions committees are looking for an authentic introduction to your child and family. The writing should reflect your personal insights and style.

## The 11 Tips (admission.org)

### General Parent Statements

1. **Demonstrate Academic Engagement**: Write one paragraph about how your child is "smart" without mentioning grades or test scores. Focus on intellectual curiosity; use anecdotes to illustrate engagement.

2. **Show Character Through Stories**: Rather than listing personality traits, use specific narratives to bring your child to life.

3. **Address Challenges and Resilience**: Discuss any difficulties your child has encountered and "how they have overcome these challenges." Schools expect parents to acknowledge obstacles rather than presenting an unrealistic portrait. Most schools can be wary if parents don't mention any challenges.

### School-Specific Questions

4. **Research the School's Values**: Take notes on the institution's mission statement and stated values from open houses and tours to create thoughtful connections.

5. **Use Specific Examples**: Draw from actual experiences with the school rather than generic praise.

6. **Verify School Names**: "Review all answers before submitting the application to make sure you have the correct name of each school."

### "Further Information" Section

7. **Disclose Learning Differences**: Use this space to explain relevant learning challenges and demonstrate how you've partnered with current educators for support.

8. **Highlight Unique Background**: Describe what diversity — of any kind — your child brings to create a more textured classroom community.

9. **Illustrate Resilience**: Share stories showing how your child navigated difficult family moments, revealing emotional strength.

10. **Fill Application Gaps**: Address anything unexplained in other sections to help admissions officers understand your student holistically.

### Follow-Up

11. **Send Thoughtful Thank You Notes**: After meetings or events, email a brief note connecting your unique child and family to the school's distinctive qualities.

## Disclosing Challenges

Per prepmatters.com (PrepMatters): "It's important to cover any challenges your child has faced and how they have overcome these challenges, as most schools ask about challenges and can be wary if parents don't mention any."

"Without oversharing, be honest about what you worry about and what you hope the specific school will provide for your child in terms of support."

## Non-Traditional Family Structures

The parent statement "is a useful avenue to spotlight unique facets of your child and family, whether it's emphasizing a diverse background or recounting challenges your family confronted and how your child responded." This explicitly suggests non-traditional family structures are appropriate essay topics.

Schools want to see that families value education, are invested in their child's development, and most importantly, "will work positively with teachers and administrators."

## Financial Constraints

No source recommends proactively disclosing financial constraints in parent essays. Financial aid applications are submitted separately through Clarity Financial. Disclosing financial stress in essays is not standard advice. However, this is not explicitly prohibited.

## Cardinal Education Guidance

- "Don't be afraid to mention your child's weaknesses"
- Answer the questions clearly and be honest
- Statements are typically 500-1500 characters long
- Use anecdotes and storytelling rather than adjective lists
- Research each school to demonstrate how your child aligns with specific values

## Core Strategy

Throughout all sections, "use examples and stories" rather than adjectives. Schools value concrete narratives that reveal character, resilience, and genuine fit. The essay is not a brochure — honesty about your family's reality is more compelling than a polished performance.
