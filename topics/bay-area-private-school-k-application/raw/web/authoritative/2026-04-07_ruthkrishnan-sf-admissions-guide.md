# How to Navigate the Private School Process in San Francisco
Source: Ruth Krishnan — ruthkrishnan.com/how-to-navigate-the-private-school-process-in-san-francisco/

## What Schools Look For at K-2 Level

Schools prioritize **developmental readiness** rather than academic skills. Key quote from admissions consultant Paula Molligan: "There isn't one school that we know of that really cares or bothers to check whether a child is reading."

Schools seek children ready for:
- Classroom collaboration and peer interaction
- Social-emotional growth and self-regulation
- Following adult directions in group settings
- Curiosity and engagement with new materials

## Critical Mistakes to Avoid

1. **Entitlement** — Appearing as though admission is guaranteed or that the family is doing the school a favor
2. **Inattention during visits** — Taking calls during interviews or appearing disengaged at open houses
3. **Dishonesty** — Hiding a child's challenges or special needs (specific quote: "Be honest about your child — they have areas of challenges and areas of strengths. If you try and cover something up about your child, that's probably not the right way to start a relationship with a school.")
4. **Poor track record** — Chronic lateness or no-shows signals disrespect for the school's time
5. **Over-documentation** — Providing unsolicited portfolios or excessive supplemental information
6. **Micromanaging** — Asking overly detailed operational questions during initial tours

## Competitive Context — SF Specific

San Francisco private schools receive approximately **7 applicants per opening** at kindergarten level. Entry points: kindergarten, 5th-6th grade, and 9th grade. Outside these years, admission depends on attrition.

## "Good Fit" — How It Actually Works

Schools consider "fit" through multiple lenses:
- Philosophy alignment: Do family values match the school's mission?
- Child fit: Will this child thrive in this learning environment?
- Community contribution: What does this family bring to the school community?

Admissions directors look at the whole family, not just the child. A family that appears unable to support the child's growth, or unable to engage honestly with challenges, signals a poor partnership for the school.

## Waitlist Reality

"Waitlists are often genuine — schools frequently create accept piles exceeding available spaces." If waitlisted, contact admissions expressing continued interest and seek recommendations from current families if possible.

## Rescheduling Assessments

Not explicitly addressed in this article, but Ruth's general guidance implies direct communication with admissions directors is always appropriate when logistical issues arise.
