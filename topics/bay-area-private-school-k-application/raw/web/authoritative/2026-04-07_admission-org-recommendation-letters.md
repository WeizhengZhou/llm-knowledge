# Recommendation Letters: Who Should You Ask — And How?
Source: Admission.org — admission.org/advice/recommendation-letters-for-private-school-applications

## Who to Ask for a Recommendation

Select individuals who genuinely know your child. Ideal recommenders include:
- Current or past teachers (most important)
- Coaches
- Activity leaders
- Trusted adults in professional or educational roles

They should be capable of articulating the child's "strengths, passions, and personality" based on recent direct observation.

## When to Ask

**Early- to mid-November is the recommended timeframe.** This allows current educators sufficient time to:
- Develop meaningful knowledge of the student in the fall term
- Write a thoughtful, specific letter
- Meet application deadlines (typically January)

Asking too late — in December or after — creates undue pressure and produces rushed, generic letters.

## How to Ask — The Approach

The article emphasizes a respectful, considerate approach:
- Acknowledge the person's time constraints
- Explain why you selected them specifically
- Provide details about requirements and deadlines
- A sample email template includes: thanks, explanation of why they were chosen, the specific school, and the submission deadline

## Following Up

- Send a reminder a few weeks before the deadline, ideally one week before the actual due date
- After submitting the application, send a thank-you note to recommenders
- If age-appropriate, the child should write the thank-you note themselves

## What Makes a Strong Letter — Key Principles

The most effective recommendation letters:
- Are written by someone who genuinely knows the child (not a famous or high-status person with minimal contact)
- Include **specific observations** and examples, not generic praise
- Describe the child's strengths, passions, and character in concrete terms
- Address both academic/cognitive strengths AND social-emotional qualities
- Mention one or two specific anecdotes that illustrate how the child approaches challenges

Example of weak language: "She is a wonderful, smart child."
Example of strong language: "When presented with a challenging puzzle she hadn't seen before, she tried three different approaches before asking for help, and when she succeeded she immediately asked if she could try a harder one."

## What NOT to Do (Critical)

From schoolfirstnyc.com complementary guidance: "Schools prefer the teachers to complete these forms completely from the teacher's perspective in their classroom. These letters are 100% confidential and a key part of the admissions process and parents' voices should not play a role in this aspect of the application." Parents should not provide bullet points, talking points, or suggested content for the teacher to include — even if well-intentioned.

## For Younger Children (K Applicants)

For kindergarten applicants, schools typically request a form from the homeroom preschool teacher. The ISSFBA standardized Confidential Recommendation Form for PreK-K enables one form submitted to multiple ISSFBA member schools. If a child has only one teacher, a previous year's teacher may write a supplemental letter.
