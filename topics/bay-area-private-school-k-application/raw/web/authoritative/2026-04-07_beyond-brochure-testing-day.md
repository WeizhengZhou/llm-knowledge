# Who's Nervous? Your Child's Private School Kindergarten Testing Day
Source: Beyond the Brochure — beyondthebrochurela.com

## What the Testing Day Experience Involves

Schools use various terms for this assessment: "visiting day," "testing day," or "play date." The experience typically includes:

- **Circle/Story Time**: Teachers read stories and ask comprehension questions
- **Written/Structured Tasks**: Assessment of letter recognition, shapes, colors, and fine motor skills
- **Play Time**: Unstructured outdoor activities where schools observe social skills, gross motor development, and ability to follow directions

## Key Observation Areas

Schools assess whether children can:
- Raise hands to answer questions
- Sit still and listen patiently
- Allow other children to speak without interrupting
- Follow basic instructions from unfamiliar adults
- Participate in play and share with peers
- Engage with activities that are new to them

## Handling Reluctant or Struggling Children — Day-of Protocol

Direct guidance from the article on what to do when children have difficulty on assessment day:

> "If your child is having a bad day when you arrive at the school, try to gently encourage him/her to separate from you and participate. If he/she refuses, politely ask the teacher to help. If that doesn't work, as a last resort, ask to come back another day. This happens occasionally and schools expect kids to have bad days."

## Rescheduling
Schools expect that children occasionally have difficult days. Rescheduling is possible — parents should ask politely and schools generally accommodate this. The assessment is one component of the overall admissions file, not the only determining factor.

## What Schools Do NOT Observe
The article emphasizes: "visiting day" or "play date" assessments do NOT test:
- Academic skills (reading, math)
- Scripted answers or rehearsed knowledge
- Perfomance under pressure

Schools are looking for developmental readiness and social-emotional indicators, not academic proficiency.

## Parent Anxiety vs. Child Anxiety
The article title deliberately addresses who is actually nervous: parents, not children. Children who are prepared by having routines maintained, adequate sleep, and who have visited the campus before the assessment tend to approach the day calmly. The adult's anxiety communicates itself to children — keeping the parent calm is itself a preparation strategy.

## Preparation Tip from Article
Taking a tour of the school the day before the assessment allows the child to see the room and meet some staff members, so the space is familiar. This reduces separation anxiety in slow-to-warm children without constituting "coaching."
