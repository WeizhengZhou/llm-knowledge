# What Is a GAI Score on WISC and WPPSI Tests?
Source: Profoundly Gifted Parenting — profoundlygiftedparenting.com

## What is the GAI?

The General Ability Index (GAI) is an alternative scoring method available on the WISC-V and WPPSI-IV intelligence tests. "The GAI score puts less emphasis on working memory and processing speed relative to the FSIQ (Full scale IQ)."

## GAI vs. FSIQ — The Core Difference

The key difference emerges when a child's working memory and processing speed scores are significantly lower than their other cognitive abilities. Example from article: when depressed WMI/PSI scores are included in the FSIQ calculation, the result is 112. Removing them and calculating GAI yields 127 — a 15-point difference. This is a meaningful gap that could place a child above or below school thresholds.

## When GAI is Calculated vs. Not

The GAI is not always automatically provided in a report. "If the working memory and processing speed scores are in line with the other scores, the FSIQ would be considered valid." However, when WMI and PSI specifically lag behind other cognitive areas, psychologists may calculate the GAI as a more accurate representation of the child's true reasoning capacity.

## What Lower WMI/PSI Scores May Indicate

Children showing depressed working memory and processing speed may have:
- Learning disabilities
- ADHD (attention affects working memory)
- Perfectionism (gifted students may work slowly to ensure accuracy)

Article notes: "gifted students may be perfectionists or high achieving" and may work slowly not because of processing issues but because of high standards for their own work.

## Why This Matters for Private School Admissions

For schools like Nueva that publish "FSIQ 130 minimum," a child with strong reasoning (VCI/VSI/FRI at 135) but ADHD-related WMI/PSI depression could have FSIQ of 120 but GAI of 133. Whether a school accepts GAI in lieu of FSIQ varies — parents must ask each school directly. Helios explicitly requests both FSIQ and GAI in submitted reports.

## Improving WMI and PSI

Brain games, puzzles, and timed activities can help. Maturation also plays a role — younger children may retest higher as attention span develops. Rule out learning disabilities first.
