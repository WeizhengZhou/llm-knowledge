# Notable Female Math Olympians and Role Models

## Maryam Mirzakhani (1977–2017)

**Why she matters:** First woman ever to win the Fields Medal (mathematics' highest honor). First Iranian to win two IMO gold medals. Demonstrates that women can reach the absolute pinnacle of mathematical achievement.

**Early life:** Born May 12, 1977, in Tehran, Iran. Did not initially intend to be a mathematician — loved reading and wanted to be a writer.

**Math olympiad achievements:**
- 1994: Won gold medal at the International Mathematical Olympiad in Hong Kong, scoring 41 out of 42 points. First Iranian woman to win an IMO gold medal.
- 1995: Won gold medal again at IMO in Toronto with a perfect score (42/42). First Iranian to achieve a perfect IMO score. First person to win two IMO gold medals representing Iran.

**Education:**
- Bachelor's degree: Sharif University of Technology, Tehran.
- PhD: Harvard University (2004).
- Positions at Princeton, then Stanford (full professor from 2009).

**Fields Medal (August 13, 2014):**
- First woman ever to win the Fields Medal (since the award was established in 1936).
- Also the first Iranian to win it.
- Cited for "the dynamics and geometry of Riemann surfaces and their moduli spaces."

**Death:** July 14, 2017, of breast cancer, age 40.

**Legacy:** UNESCO designated May 12 (her birthday) as "World Women in Mathematics Day." Her story is widely used to inspire girls in competitive math programs globally.

## Melanie Matchett Wood (born 1981)

**Why she matters:** First American woman to qualify for the US IMO team. First woman to win the Putnam Fellowship. Demonstrates that a girl can break through the highest barrier in American competitive math.

**High school achievements:**
- Student at Park Tudor School in Indianapolis.
- Age 16: Became the first female American to make the US International Mathematical Olympiad team.
- 1998 IMO: Earned silver medal.
- 1999 IMO: Earned silver medal again.

**Undergraduate:**
- Duke University, graduated 2003.
- 2002: First American woman (and second woman overall in history) to be named a Putnam Fellow.

**Graduate and academic career:**
- Gates Cambridge Scholarship, Fulbright fellowship, NSF graduate fellowship.
- Cambridge University (2003–2004).
- PhD: Princeton University, 2009 (advisor: Manjul Bhargava, himself an IMO gold medalist and later Fields Medal winner).
- Positions at Stanford, University of Wisconsin, UC Berkeley.
- Current: Professor of Mathematics, Harvard University.

**Awards:**
- Morgan Prize (2004) — first woman to win this award for undergraduate research.
- AWM-Microsoft Research Prize in Algebra and Number Theory (2018).
- NSF Alan T. Waterman Award (2021).
- MacArthur Fellowship ("Genius Grant") (2022).
- National Academy of Sciences membership (2025).

**Research area:** Number theory, arithmetic statistics.

## Alison Miller

- Represented the United States at IMO 2004.
- Won a gold medal.
- Studied mathematics at Harvard University.
- Completed PhD in mathematics at Princeton University.

## Sherry Gong

- Represented the United States at IMO 2005 and 2007.
- Won gold medal in 2007.
- Scored over 100 in Harvard's legendary problem-solving course Math 55.
- Completed PhD at MIT in mathematics.

## Historical Statistics: Women at the IMO

- From 1959 to 2021: 43 female gold medalists (3.3%) out of 1,338 total gold medals.
- In 2020: Only 1 female gold medalist at the entire IMO.
- The gender gap at the IMO level is extreme — motivating the creation of EGMO as an alternative pathway.

## US EGMO Champions (Recent)

The US is the leading nation in EGMO history:
- All-time medal table through 2025: 39 gold, 12 silver, 5 bronze.
- 2025 US EGMO team won 4 gold medals (all members); finished 2nd overall.
- 2025 team: Shruti Arun, Hannah Fox, Alansha Jiang, Angela Liu (all age 17).

## Significance for Parents and Coaches

Research shows that increasing female role models measurably reduces stereotype threat effects on girls' performance. Specific, named female role models who succeeded through competition math — particularly those who faced adversity or were "firsts" — are more effective than abstract statements about gender equity.
