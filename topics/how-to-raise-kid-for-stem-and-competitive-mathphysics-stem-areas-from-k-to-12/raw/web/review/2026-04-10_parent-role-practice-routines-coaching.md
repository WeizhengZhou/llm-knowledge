# Parent Role, Practice Routines, and Coaching Decisions

## The Parent Role by Stage

### Elementary (K-5): Lead and Facilitate
- Parents identify interest, introduce enrichment materials
- Read Beast Academy together, play math games (SET, Prime Climb, chess)
- Create low-pressure exposure: puzzles, number talks, asking "why" questions
- Enroll in program if available (Beast Academy online, Singapore Math, RSM, AoPS Academy)
- Avoid: drilling, timed pressure, comparing to other kids

### Middle School (6-8): Facilitate and Support
- Parents arrange program enrollment (AoPS courses, RSM, AlphaStar)
- Provide logistics support (transportation, schedule protection)
- Encourage without monitoring every session
- Learn enough to have math conversations, not to teach directly
- Register child for AMC 8, MathCounts, MOEMS
- Avoid: tutoring the student yourself (causes tension), grade-tracking obsession

### High School (9-12): Step Back, Enable
- Students should drive their own preparation at this stage
- Parent role: financial support, logistics, emotional regulation during stress
- Avoid: monitoring hours studied, comparing to peers, pushing specific competitions
- Trust the student's judgment on when to rest

### Key Principle
"The student-parent tutoring relationship doesn't work well for mathematics. Children try to impress parents and live up to expectations, creating math anxiety." (Alexander Tutoring)

Parents who are strongest helpers: create an environment of safety, normalize failure, celebrate curiosity rather than scores.

## Private Tutor vs. Group Classes vs. Self-Study

### When Private Tutoring Adds Value
- Student is plateau-ing at a specific level (e.g., consistently scoring 100-110 on AMC 12 but not breaking through to AIME)
- Student needs personalized feedback on proof-writing
- Geographic isolation from group programs
- Student has specific weaknesses that group classes don't address

### When Group Classes Are Better
- Building peer community (motivating, normalizing difficulty)
- Competitive benchmark with peers at similar level
- Cost-efficiency (private tutoring $100-300/hour vs. group $50-100/month for programs)
- AoPS online, AlphaStar, RSM competition track, local math circles

### When Self-Study Works Best
- Motivated, self-directed learners (common among top competitors)
- AoPS books + problem archives + AoPS forums is sufficient for most students to reach AIME level
- USAMO-level students often mix self-study with targeted coaching

### Research on Top Competitors
Most USAMO qualifiers and IMO team members report learning primarily through:
1. Self-study (AoPS books, past contest problems)
2. Online community (AoPS forums, finding peers at similar level)
3. Occasional coaching or summer program (Ross, PROMYS, Mathcamp, MOP)
Private tutoring is less common among the very top competitors than expected.

## Practice Routines by Level

### AMC 8 / MathCounts Level (Middle School, Beginner)
- **Weekly time:** 3-5 hours/week is sufficient
- **Format:** 30-45 min problem solving sessions, 3-4x/week
- **Content:** AMC 8 past tests, MATHCOUNTS Sprint/Target rounds, AoPS Introduction series
- **Key habit:** Review all wrong answers thoroughly — understand why the correct answer is correct
- **Red flag:** Grinding through problems without review

### AMC 10/12 / Early AIME Level (Middle/High School)
- **Weekly time:** 5-10 hours/week for serious preparation
- **Format:** Timed practice tests + untimed deeper problem work
- **Content:** AMC 10/12 past tests (25 years of free archives), AoPS Intermediate series, AIME past tests
- **Key habit:** After a timed test, spend equal time reviewing — understand every problem you got wrong AND every problem you guessed right
- **Community:** AoPS forums — post solutions and read others' approaches

### AIME Qualifier → USAMO Level (High School, Serious)
- **Weekly time:** 10-20 hours/week (intense periods)
- **Format:** Mix of timed practice (AIME, past USAMOs) and untimed deep work on olympiad problems (1-3 hour per problem sessions)
- **Content:** AoPS WOOT, OTIS (Evan Chen's program), past USAMO/IMO problems, Intermediate to Olympiad AoPS books
- **Key habit:** Spend significant time stuck on a problem before reading the solution. "One hour on a single problem" is the recommended minimum for olympiad-level work
- **Proof practice:** USAMTS (one month, untimed, feedback) is ideal for developing proof-writing skills

### USAMO → IMO Level
- Reported preparation from USAMO qualifiers and IMO team members:
  - 15-25 hours/week of dedicated problem work during peak season
  - Daily work on hard problems (morning session, 1-2 hours before school)
  - Summer programs (MOP, Ross, Mathcamp) provide intensive training and peer community
  - International problems: IMO shortlist, Balkan MO, China MO for additional challenge

## Healthy Schedule Model

### Elementary Student (3-5 hours/week enrichment)
- 30-45 min Beast Academy or math games: 4x/week
- 1 competition prep session/month (practice contest)
- No elimination of other activities

### Middle School (MATHCOUNTS / AMC 8 level, 5-7 hours/week)
- AoPS or RSM class: 1.5-2 hours/week (program)
- Self-study practice: 3-4 hours/week
- Math circle or similar: 1 hour/week (optional)
- Sports, music, socializing: protected time (not sacrificed)

### High School (AMC 12 / AIME level, 8-12 hours/week)
- AoPS course or AlphaStar: 2-3 hours/week
- Daily problem work: 1-2 hours/day (30-60 min minimum even on busy days)
- Weekly timed practice test review: 1-2 hours
- Sleep 8+ hours: non-negotiable for performance

### During Competition Season (AMC November, AIME February-March)
- Increase practice to peak levels for 4-6 weeks before exam
- Reduce after exam — allow recovery
- Don't maintain peak intensity year-round (burnout risk)

## What Families of Successful Competitors Do Differently

1. **Long runway:** Started enrichment in elementary school, not middle school
2. **Student-driven:** Student asked to do competitions, parent facilitated
3. **Balance maintained:** Sports/music/social protected alongside math
4. **Community-connected:** Enrolled in programs (AoPS, summer camps) where student found math peers
5. **Low parental anxiety:** Parents did not obsess over scores or rankings
6. **Allowed failure:** Student learned from wrong answers rather than parents rushing to re-teach
