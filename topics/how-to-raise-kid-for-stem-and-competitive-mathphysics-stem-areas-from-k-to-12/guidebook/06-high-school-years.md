# Chapter 6: The High School Years — Grades 9–12

High school is when most parents think the real competition training begins. But students who reach USAMO almost universally say the work that got them there happened before 9th grade.

This isn't discouraging news — it's clarifying. What it means is that the high school years, for a well-prepared student, aren't about catching up. They're about converting mathematical foundations into competition results, adding proof-writing and research skills, and making real decisions about how much of this they want to pursue. For a student just discovering competition math in 9th grade, it's not too late — but the path looks different, and it's worth being honest about what's realistic.

This chapter covers what the high school competition track actually looks like, week by week and year by year.

---

## What 9th Grade Looks Like: Two Starting Points

### The well-prepared student

A student who enters 9th grade having completed the AoPS Introduction series, qualified for MATHCOUNTS regionals, and taken a serious AMC 10 in 8th grade is ready to move directly into AMC 10/12 competition work. For them, 9th grade is about consolidating Intermediate Algebra, starting Introduction to Geometry if they haven't completed it, and taking their first AMC 10 in November with the genuine intention of qualifying for AIME. Whether they make it in 9th grade or 10th is less important than whether the work is consistent.

These students also typically begin USAMTS in the fall of 9th grade — a proof-based alternative competition that develops proof-writing skills they'll need if they ever reach USAMO. More on USAMTS below.

### The student starting fresh

A student who enters 9th grade with strong school math but minimal competition experience is not starting from zero — but they're starting from behind relative to peers who've been training for three years. The right move is not to immediately jump into AMC 10 preparation. The right move is to assess the actual skill gap honestly and fill it efficiently.

If the student has solid algebra but hasn't seen competition-style problems, starting with AoPS Introduction to Algebra and AMC 8 past tests gives them both content review and exposure to the competition format. Many students catch up to an AIME-qualifying level within two to three years of dedicated work if they start in 9th grade. What they won't do is qualify for USAMO, absent extraordinary ability. That's a realistic constraint worth knowing.

## The AMC 10/12 Strategy: Which Test, When, How Many Attempts

The AMC 10 is open to students in grade 10 and below (age under 17.5). The AMC 12 covers more advanced mathematics, including trigonometry and advanced algebra, and is open through grade 12. Both are 25-question, 75-minute tests, scoring 6 points for correct, 1.5 for blank, 0 for wrong.

The strategic questions for each year are: which test do you take, and which sitting (A or B)?

For 9th and 10th graders targeting USAJMO, take the AMC 10. USAJMO qualification uses AMC 10 scores, not AMC 12. A student in 10th grade who takes AMC 12 and qualifies for USAMO is invited to USAMO rather than USAJMO — but a student targeting USAJMO from the AMC 10 route should not switch to AMC 12 prematurely.

For 11th and 12th graders targeting USAMO, take the AMC 12. The AMC 12 has a higher AIME qualification threshold (top 5% vs. top 2.5% for AMC 10), but the USAMO qualifying index uses the AMC 12 score, and high AMC 12 scores can partially compensate for a middling AIME score.

Both A and B versions of each test are offered in November — A in early November, B about a week later. Experienced coaches advise taking both when possible: if you perform poorly on the A date, the B date is a second chance. The better score is the one that counts for qualification. Taking both is standard practice among serious competitors.

## AIME: What Qualifying Means and What It Demands

AIME qualification — finishing in the top 2.5% of AMC 10 takers or top 5% of AMC 12 takers — is the first meaningful filter in the US math competition pipeline. Approximately 6,000–7,000 students qualify annually out of roughly 300,000 who take AMC 10/12. It's a real accomplishment and a genuine threshold.

The AIME itself is a 15-question, 3-hour exam with integer answers in the range 000–999. There are no multiple choice options; you can't guess your way to a score. And critically: calculus is excluded, but the problem style demands deep algebraic, geometric, and combinatorial thinking rather than content breadth.

AIME scores range from 0 to 15. A score of 5 is typical for first-time qualifiers. A score of 10 or above is genuinely strong. A score of 12 or above puts you in serious contention for USAMO qualification, depending on your AMC score.

The 2025-2026 qualifying index formula changed significantly: USAMO/USAJMO qualification is now based on (AIME score × 20) + AMC score. This change substantially increases the weight of the AIME relative to previous years. Under the old formula, a 130 AMC score plus a 9 AIME score might have been competitive. Under the new formula, the AIME score matters much more — strong AMC performance alone won't carry you.

According to the competition pipeline guide, students may take the AIME only once per cycle, even if they qualified through both AMC A and B dates.

How do you prepare for AIME? The primary method is past AIME problems — all freely available from the MAA website — combined with AoPS Intermediate series books. AoPS WOOT (Worldwide Online Olympiad Training), priced at $845–895/year, is designed for students who have already reached AIME qualification and are working toward USAMO-level problems. Do not buy WOOT before you've qualified for AIME; the material will be impenetrable.

## The USAMO Cliff: Honesty About How Few Get There

Approximately 500 students qualify for USAMO or USAJMO combined each year — the top roughly 11% of AIME participants. Of 300,000 students who start at AMC 10/12, fewer than 0.2% reach this level.

This framing matters because many families enter the competition pipeline with the implicit goal of USAMO, not realizing how narrow that funnel is. An AIME qualifier in the top third of their state is already a legitimately strong student. USAMO qualification represents a different tier entirely.

The USAMO also requires something that AIME doesn't: proof writing. USAMO problems require full mathematical proofs, graded by expert judges. A student who can score 10 on AIME but has never written a proof will find USAMO completely foreign — not because they lack the mathematical ability, but because proof-writing is a distinct skill that has to be trained separately.

The typical profile of a USAMO qualifier: they began training seriously by 7th or 8th grade, consistently scored in the top percentile on AMC 10/12 through multiple cycles, reached AIME by 9th or 10th grade, scored 12+ on AIME by 10th or 11th grade, and spent one to two years doing serious proof practice through USAMTS, math circles, or programs like PROMYS or Ross.

An AIME score of 8 in 10th grade with no proof training is not "close to USAMO." It's one to two years of hard work from being in contention. That's a meaningful distance. Families who are honest about it make better decisions about how to allocate time and effort.

## Starting Physics: When F=ma Makes Sense

The physics competition pipeline runs in parallel to the math pipeline, and it has a shorter runway. The primary entry point is the F=ma exam, administered by the American Association of Physics Teachers each January. F=ma is a 75-minute, 25-question multiple-choice exam covering classical mechanics — the same physics you see in AP Physics C: Mechanics, though at a deeper problem-solving level.

F=ma qualifiers sit for the USAPhO (US Physics Olympiad) in April, a 3.5-hour exam with more complex, multi-step problems. Top USAPhO performers may be selected for the US Physics Team, leading to IPhO (International Physics Olympiad) in July.

Most competition math instructors recommend starting F=ma preparation no earlier than 10th grade, and only after completing algebra-based physics through a course or self-study. The mathematical prerequisites — algebra, basic trigonometry, and comfort with vectors — are accessible by 10th grade for most students in the math pipeline. Calculus-based mechanics (used at USAPhO level) is where Kleppner and Kolenkow's *Introduction to Mechanics* becomes the primary text.

The key point: a student who is currently in 10th or 11th grade and has done zero physics competitions still has two chances at F=ma. The pipeline is shorter, which means a later start is more recoverable than it would be in math. Many families who've done this report that a student who dedicates one focused year to F=ma preparation — working David Morin's mechanics problems systematically — can reach qualification without years of prior physics competition training.

## USAMTS: The Alternative Track for Proof-Thinkers

The USA Mathematical Talent Search is a free, proof-based competition that runs three rounds per year, each with 5 problems and over a month to solve them. Any resources are allowed except help from another person. Solutions are submitted in writing and graded with personalized written feedback.

According to its official website, USAMTS is administered by the Art of Problem Solving Foundation and supported by the National Security Agency. A score of approximately 68/75 or higher can qualify for AIME as an alternative to AMC 10/12.

USAMTS is particularly valuable for students who are mathematically strong but perform badly under timed pressure, or who learn proof-writing most effectively when given extended time to think. The written feedback from graders is essentially one-on-one coaching on mathematical writing — a resource that's otherwise expensive and hard to access.

For students aiming at USAMO, USAMTS is not a replacement for AMC preparation but a complement to it. The proof-writing development from USAMTS directly transfers to USAMO problem sets.

## ARML: The Team Experience

ARML — American Regions Mathematics League — is an annual national team competition held in late May or early June, simultaneously at five US sites. Teams of 15 represent geographic regions. Competition rounds include a Team Round (20 minutes, 10 problems), a Power Question (a 1-hour proof exploration — the most USAMO-like round in any team competition), Individual Rounds, and a Relay Round.

ARML is the most socially rich competition in the high school calendar. It's a full day, with a team of students who've often trained together for months. The Power Question is genuinely hard and rewards mathematical maturity over speed. According to Wikipedia, 2025–2026 competition sites include University of Iowa, Penn State, University of Nevada Reno, Western Carolina University, and Saint Anselm College.

Students join ARML through their regional or state math team, which has its own qualifying process. If your child's school participates in a regional math team, ARML is worth the effort — not because of what it does for college applications, but because competing alongside 15 teammates at a national meet is an experience that standalone AMC tests can't replicate.

## What Serious High School Training Actually Looks Like

For a student working seriously toward AIME qualification (or beyond), a realistic week in 10th or 11th grade might look like:

**Daily (30–45 minutes):** Alcumus at intermediate level, or 3–5 problems from whatever Intermediate series book they're in. This is habit work.

**Two 90-minute sessions per week:** Working through AIME past problems at this point — systematically, by year, tracking which topics feel weak. A student who works through five years of AIME I and II problems and goes back to read the solutions for every problem they missed has done serious preparation.

**Weekends:** WOOT problem sets for students who have enrolled; proof-writing practice via USAMTS rounds; occasionally a mock AMC 12 under timed conditions.

**Summers:** Ross, PROMYS, or Mathcamp for students who are accepted. Those programs are discussed in Chapter 7. For students not attending residential programs, AwesomeMath summer online courses are the best structured alternative.

The students who reach USAMO typically describe one thing that's easy to overlook: they worked through problems they couldn't solve. Not skimming solutions when stuck, but genuinely attempting a problem for 30–60 minutes before looking at hints. That patience — sitting with mathematical discomfort — is what training is actually developing.

## Science Research: When to Start an ISEF Project

For students interested in the science research track — Regeneron ISEF, Regeneron STS, or RSI — the junior year is too late to start thinking about it. The timeline runs roughly as follows:

- **9th or 10th grade:** Identify a research mentor (university professor, graduate student, or science teacher willing to supervise original work)
- **10th or early 11th grade:** Begin the research project, collect data, iterate
- **11th grade:** Complete the primary work, write it up for regional and state science fair
- **11th or 12th grade:** Submit to ISEF, STS, or use the work as the foundation for RSI application

The ISEF and STS tracks require a type of work that competition math doesn't develop: patience with open-ended problems, experimental thinking, and the ability to write up original findings. Students who have done well in competition math don't automatically transfer those skills to research. Some do; many find it harder than expected.

The students who succeed in research science in high school are usually those who found an area of genuine curiosity — not the area they thought colleges would approve of — and pursued it long enough to have something real to say.

## Managing School + Competitions + Everything Else

The honest answer: something has to give, and the families that figure out what can give before they're in crisis do better.

A student taking 7 AP courses while competing seriously in math and doing science research and playing three sports is scheduled for burnout. The math is simple. The choices are not — these students are often extremely capable, and saying no to anything feels like closing a door.

Competition math instructors who work with high school students often observe that the ones who perform best at AMC 12 level are those who've chosen depth over breadth in their extracurricular lives. One serious math focus plus one non-academic activity plus manageable school commitments. Not five things at 80%.

The competition calendar itself creates natural compression in the fall semester: AMC 10A/12A in early November, AMC 10B/12B a week later, AIME in February, USAMO in March. That's five months of high-stakes preparation landing on top of school and college application season. Students who haven't set boundaries on their schedule before this stretch typically find themselves exhausted when it matters most.
