# Chapter 12: The Real Cost — What Competitive STEM Actually Costs

*You will know what this path costs at every level — from enrichment-only to olympiad track — and where financial aid actually exists.*

---

Nobody talks about the cost of competitive STEM preparation honestly. The forums are full of advice about AoPS and Ross, but rarely the price tags attached. Parents who are newer to this world absorb a set of implicit norms — get the AoPS books, go to a summer program, consider private coaching — without a clear picture of what they are signing up for financially, or the honest acknowledgment that a large portion of this is optional.

Here are the real numbers, organized by what level of engagement you are actually pursuing. And here, prominently, is the most important thing in this chapter: you can take a student from zero to AIME-level for under $500 total, using resources that exist right now, for free.

---

## Tier 1: Enrichment Only ($0–$500/year)

At this level, the student is participating in competitions as intellectual enrichment — AMC 8 or AMC 10 as a 6th or 7th grader, perhaps MATHCOUNTS at the school level, with no intensive preparation program and no formal coaching.

The costs are minimal:

- AMC registration: typically covered by the school, or $10-30 if paid directly
- MATHCOUNTS competition: usually free or nominal school fee
- A book or two: AoPS Introduction to Algebra or Introduction to Number Theory runs $59-63 each

A parent who buys two AoPS books and downloads ten years of past AMC and MATHCOUNTS exams from the official websites (both free) has equipped a motivated student through the AMC 8 and into early AMC 10 territory. That is the Tier 1 reality.

The free resources that cover this tier completely:

All past AMC 8, AMC 10, and AMC 12 exams are available at maa.org at no cost. All AIME past exams are available there as well. MATHCOUNTS handbooks, which contain competition problems and preparation materials, are available at mathcounts.org. Alcumus — AoPS's adaptive problem system — is free. A student who works through Alcumus systematically through middle school has a meaningful preparation base, at zero cost.

---

## Tier 2: Competition Track ($500–$3,000/year)

This is a student who is taking AMC 10 or AMC 12 seriously, working toward AIME qualification, possibly in a math circle or an AoPS online course, and perhaps attending a regional competition.

Typical expenditures at this level:

- AoPS courses: $415-$895 per course. A student might take one or two per year. AMC 10 Preparation, AIME B, Number Theory — these are the courses that matter at this level. One course per year is $500-$900.
- AoPS books: the full Introduction series (Algebra, Counting and Probability, Number Theory, Geometry) is roughly $240 for all four. The Intermediate series adds another $240 or so.
- Math circle: often free through universities or math departments; some private circles charge $200-$500/semester.
- Competition travel: local competitions are usually free or low-cost. State MATHCOUNTS, if the student qualifies, involves a day trip.

A realistic Tier 2 budget: one AoPS course, the core book set, and occasional competition fees. That gets you to roughly $1,000-$1,500 per year. You can trim that significantly by substituting AoPS books for the course in some years — a motivated student with good books and past exams can cover a lot of this material independently.

One note on math learning centers: Kumon, Mathnasium, and similar programs run $150-$200 per month per subject, or $1,800-$2,400 per year. These are designed for grade-level acceleration and remediation, not competition math preparation. They are not the right tool for this track, and the cost-to-benefit ratio for competition preparation is low. A student at this level is better served by AoPS books and Alcumus.

## Tier 3: Serious Competitor ($3,000–$8,000/year)

This is a student working toward strong AIME performance — scores of 8 or above — potentially aiming for USAJMO, and attending one selective summer program.

The summer program is the primary cost driver at this tier. The major programs for 2025-2026:

- Ross Mathematics Program: approximately $7,500 for the six-week residential program
- Canada/USA Mathcamp: approximately $7,500
- Hampshire College Summer Studies in Mathematics (HCSSiM): approximately $7,208
- MathILy: approximately $5,300
- AwesomeMath Summer Program: $1,275-$1,575 per course (shorter, modular)

Add year-round expenses — two AoPS courses, possibly WOOT (Worldwide Online Olympiad Training, $845-$895) — and the annual number for a student attending one major summer program runs $8,000-$10,000 in the summer program year, somewhat less in off years.

**The financial aid picture is meaningfully better than most families expect.**

PROMYS (Program in Mathematics for Young Scientists) is free for families with household income under $80,000. This is not a partial scholarship; it is full tuition, room, and board at no cost. Above $80,000, need-based aid is available at all income levels. Apply regardless of whether you think you qualify — the aid formula is worth running.

Canada/USA Mathcamp is free for US and Canadian families with household income under $100,000, with need-based aid available above that threshold.

PROMYS and Mathcamp together represent a genuinely free Tier 3 summer program experience for families under those income thresholds. A student who attends PROMYS or Mathcamp on full aid and supplements with AoPS books and free resources has a Tier 3 preparation at Tier 1 cost.

RSI (Research Science Institute) is completely free — tuition, room, and board — for all admitted students. It is also extraordinarily selective (roughly 2.5-3% acceptance rate), so not a planning assumption, but worth noting: the most prestigious summer research program in the country costs nothing.

MOP (Mathematical Olympiad Program, summer training for top USAMO performers) is also free and invitation-only. Students who make it to MOP are not paying for the privilege.

## Tier 4: Top-Tier With Coaching ($8,000–$20,000+/year)

This tier reflects students with realistic USAMO aspirations who are working with private coaches and attending multiple selective programs.

Private mathematical coaching at this level — from mathematicians who have themselves competed at olympiad level — runs $150-$400 per hour, depending on the coach's background and location. A student working with a coach for two hours per week for 40 weeks per year is spending $12,000-$32,000 on coaching alone. Most families at this tier work with a coach for less frequency — one hour per week, or intensive sessions during specific preparation periods — but the numbers add up.

One aggregator source estimates Tier 4 annual costs at $8,000-$20,000+, and that estimate is roughly consistent with real patterns: a selective summer program ($7,500), WOOT ($895), a semester of coaching, and competition travel. In a year with multiple programs or intensive coaching, $20,000+ is not unusual.

A few things worth knowing at this tier:

Most students at USAMO-qualifier level got there without private coaching. The AoPS community includes many self-taught olympiad competitors who relied on books, forums, and summer programs rather than individual coaches. Private coaching accelerates progress for some students; it is not a prerequisite for reaching the top.

The marginal return on spending decreases rapidly above the one-summer-program level. A student who attends one selective summer program (Mathcamp, Ross, PROMYS) and uses free resources plus AoPS books for the rest of the year will develop comparably to a student who attends two programs and adds a private coach — in many cases. The ceiling is the student's mathematical talent and intrinsic motivation, not the preparation budget.

## What Is Actually Free

This deserves its own section, because the free resources available today are genuinely excellent.

**Competition practice:**
Every past AMC 8, AMC 10, AMC 12, AIME, USAMO, and USAJMO problem is freely available at maa.org. Every past F=ma (the first round of the USAPhO pathway) is at aapt.org. Past IPhO (International Physics Olympiad) problems are at ipho.info. MATHCOUNTS competition handbooks, including the School, Chapter, State, and National levels, are freely downloadable.

**Structured learning:**
Alcumus (AoPS) is free and adaptive, matching difficulty to a student's current level. Expii, founded by Po-Shen Loh (former USA IMO team coach), is free and covers roughly grades 5-9 in an interactive format. AoPS Wiki contains guides, solution discussions, and competition archives. USAMTS (USA Mathematical Talent Search) is a free proof-based competition that provides personalized written feedback on solutions — useful for developing proof skills, with no fee.

**Advanced study materials:**
Evan Chen's OTIS Excerpts, covering olympiad topics from the author of several standard olympiad resources, are free at web.evanchen.cc. Kevin Zhou's Physics Olympiad Handouts, a comprehensive set covering the IPhO syllabus, are free at knzhou.github.io. MIT OpenCourseWare provides university-level courses at no cost — Linear Algebra, Real Analysis, Abstract Algebra — for students who have exhausted competition-level material and want to go further.

**Library books:**
AoPS Introduction series books, Zeitz's "The Art and Craft of Problem Solving," Engel's "Problem Solving Strategies" — all available in university library systems, often with interlibrary loan options. A student in a city with a decent public library system can access most of the standard competition preparation literature at no cost.

A family that uses only free resources, plus borrowed books, can prepare a student through AIME level effectively. The paid programs — AoPS courses, summer programs — are genuine value-adds. They are not prerequisites.

## The Hidden Costs

Beyond program fees, there are costs that rarely appear in planning conversations.

**Competition registration and travel.** AMC registration is often free through a school; if not, it is $10-30. MATHCOUNTS state and national competitions involve travel. For students who qualify for AIME in person (schools administer it), there is usually no separate fee. For students who qualify for USAMO and attend the proof-writing competition, travel to the site (or costs for the remote version) add up.

**Testing fees.** SAT, ACT, AP exams — relevant to the broader high school picture if the student is being considered for acceleration or dual enrollment programs. Not competition-specific, but often part of the same family budget.

**Technology and subscriptions.** AoPS has a paid Alcumus Pro tier; the free version is sufficient for most purposes. Wolfram Alpha, Desmos, and similar tools are free. A basic graphing calculator ($100-150) is needed for some competitions but not for most olympiad-level work (olympiad problems do not use calculators).

**Time.** This is the most significant hidden cost, and it does not appear in any budget. One review source estimates practice hours at AMC 8 level as 2-4 hours per week, AMC 10/12 at 5-10 hours per week, AIME at 8-15 hours per week, and USAMO-level at 15-25+ hours per week. These are estimates from an aggregator site rather than empirical data, but they are plausible ranges based on what the preparation actually requires.

At 8-15 hours per week (AIME-level preparation), a student cannot maintain a second serious time commitment — a varsity sport, a musical instrument practiced at performance level — without something giving. Usually that something is sleep, social time, or both.

At 15-25+ hours per week (USAMO-track preparation), competition math has become the student's primary activity outside of school. The college application will reflect this accurately. That is fine if the student genuinely wants this; it is a meaningful sacrifice if they are doing it because they feel they have to.

## The Opportunity Cost Framing

Financial cost and time cost are related but distinct, and it helps to think about them separately.

A student can pursue AMC 10/12 preparation at 5-10 hours per week with almost no financial outlay, using free resources and borrowed books. The financial cost is trivial; the time cost is real but manageable alongside a full life.

A student pursuing AIME-level training at 8-15 hours per week, possibly with an AoPS course, is spending real time and modest money. The financial cost is meaningful for many families — $1,000-2,000/year — but not prohibitive. The time cost is significant: this student is specializing.

A student on the USAMO track at 15-25+ hours per week, attending a selective summer program, is spending $7,000-15,000 in a summer program year, and the time cost is the dominant factor: this is the student's life, not an extracurricular.

The question to ask at each level is not just "can we afford this?" but "is this the right use of this period of the student's life?" The financial cost is often manageable; the opportunity cost — other interests not pursued, other experiences not had, the range of a childhood — is real and does not refund.

## Financial Aid Strategy, Step by Step

1. **Start with free resources.** Alcumus, past exams, library books. Most students through AIME level do not need anything else to make strong progress.

2. **Invest in books before courses.** The AoPS Introduction series (roughly $240 for four books) plus the Intermediate series covers most AMC-to-AIME material. One book per six months is a reasonable pace for most students. Books before online courses is the right order.

3. **Apply for summer program aid regardless of income.** PROMYS is free under $80K; Mathcamp is free under $100K. Every other major program has need-based aid at some level. The formulas vary by program and are not always income-based in the way you expect. Apply. Fill out the aid form. Do not self-screen out.

4. **Avoid private tutoring before AIME level.** The research on this is directional but consistent across parent community reports: self-study with AoPS books outperforms tutoring for most students through AMC 10/12 level. Tutoring adds marginal value at the AIME-to-USAMO transition and above. Before that, the student needs more practice, not more instruction.

5. **Avoid multiple simultaneous programs.** Two AoPS courses in the same semester, plus a math circle, plus competition prep — this is not additive. It tends to reduce the quality of engagement with each. One serious thing at a time, done well, produces better results than three things done partially.
