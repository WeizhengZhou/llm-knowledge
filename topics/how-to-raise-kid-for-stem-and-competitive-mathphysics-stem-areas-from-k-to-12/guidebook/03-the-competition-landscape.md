# Chapter 3: The Competition Landscape — Your Map of What's Out There

---

What is AMC, and why does everyone seem to know about it except me?

If you've recently stumbled into the world of competitive math — maybe a teacher mentioned it, or your kid's friend is apparently "doing AIME" — you're allowed to feel disoriented. The acronym soup is genuinely thick: AMC, AIME, USAMO, USAJMO, MATHCOUNTS, MOEMS, ARML, F=ma, USAPhO, ISEF, STS. Everyone in this world speaks in initials and assumes you've been here from birth.

You haven't. So let's draw the map first.

There are three main tracks: competition math, physics competitions, and science research competitions. They're related — they draw from a similar pool of mathematically strong students — but they have different structures, different timelines, and different skills they reward. A student doesn't have to pick one permanently, especially before high school. But it helps enormously to understand all three before making any decisions.

---

## Track 1: The Math Competition Pipeline

The math competition pipeline is the most established and most populated track. The pathway runs: AMC 8 → AMC 10/12 → AIME → USAMO/USAJMO → MOP → IMO.

Think of it as a funnel. Roughly 300,000 students enter at the AMC 10/12 level each year. About 6,000–7,000 advance to the AIME. Around 500 qualify for USAMO or USAJMO. Approximately 60–80 attend MOP. Six students represent the US at the International Mathematical Olympiad. Fewer than 0.2% of students who sit the AMC ultimately qualify for USAMO.

Those numbers are not meant to be discouraging. They're meant to help you calibrate what "doing well" looks like at each stage — and to make clear that most students engage meaningfully with competition math without ever coming close to the national stage. The IMO is for a tiny number of exceptional students. Qualifying for AIME is already a notable achievement. Getting a solid AMC 8 score in sixth grade and learning to love math problems is the goal for most families reading this.

### The AMC 8: Where Most Students Start

The AMC 8 is administered by the Mathematical Association of America (MAA). It's 25 questions, 40 minutes, multiple choice, for students in grade 8 and below. As of the 2025–2026 cycle, it runs during a window in late January.

No penalty for wrong answers. Problems cover pre-algebra, introductory geometry, basic number theory, and counting. It's the right first formal competition for a student who has started competition math enrichment — and a useful diagnostic if you're not sure yet.

What does a good score look like? A score of 20 or above is a signal of genuine readiness for AMC 10 preparation. A score of 15–19 is respectable and suggests there's more to learn, not that the child lacks ability. A student who scores 12 on their first AMC 8 having aced every school math test should not conclude they're bad at math — they should conclude they've been doing a different activity called school math, and competition math is something you have to learn separately. We'll come back to this distinction at length in Chapter 4.

### The AMC 10 and AMC 12: The High School Gateway

The AMC 10 and AMC 12 are both 25-question, 75-minute multiple-choice tests. The AMC 10 is for students in grade 10 and below (under age 17.5); the AMC 12 is for grade 12 and below.

The scoring system: 6 points for a correct answer, 1.5 points for a blank, 0 for wrong. Maximum score: 150. There are two versions of each exam — an "A" version and a "B" version, given a week apart — and students can take either or both.

AIME qualification requires finishing in the top 2.5% of AMC 10 participants, or the top 5% of AMC 12 participants. In the 2025–2026 cycle, for the AMC 10A, the qualifying threshold was a score of 100.5 or above. Approximately 6,000–7,000 students qualify for AIME each year.

One practical detail that surprises families every November: AMC tests must be proctored through a registered school or testing center. Your child's school has to be a participating site. Many parents discover this problem when AMC 10/12 registration is closing. Check your school's participation status in September, not October.

A second surprise: which exam to take matters for which path you're aiming at. USAJMO qualification uses AMC 10 scores. USAMO qualification uses AMC 12 scores. A 9th or 10th grader targeting USAJMO should take the AMC 10, not the AMC 12 — even if the AMC 12 feels more prestigious.

### The AIME: What Qualifying Means

Qualifying for AIME — making it past the AMC cutoff — is the first major milestone in the pipeline. It means your child is in the top 2.5% of AMC 10 test-takers in the country.

The AIME itself is 15 questions, 3 hours, and unlike the AMC, there are no answer choices. Answers are integers between 000 and 999. You have to actually solve the problem; no educated guessing. The problems cover the same subject areas as the AMC but at significantly higher difficulty.

Two AIME dates are offered each year (AIME I and AIME II), but students may take it only once per cycle. If a student qualified on both the A and B versions of the AMC, both AMC scores are combined with their one AIME score, and the better index is used.

Some students qualify for AIME at the end of middle school. More commonly, a student qualified through sustained preparation in high school finds it to be their first experience of genuinely hard math — problems that don't yield to the same pattern-matching that works on AMC. That's the point. It's supposed to be hard.

### USAMO and USAJMO: The National Stage

Approximately the top 11% of AIME participants — roughly 500 students combined — qualify for either the USA Mathematical Olympiad (USAMO) or the USA Junior Mathematical Olympiad (USAJMO).

The qualifying formula changed for the 2025–2026 cycle. Under the new formula, the qualifying index is: AIME score × 20 + AMC score. (The previous formula used AIME × 10.) This change significantly increases the weight of the AIME relative to AMC performance. A student who qualified under the old formula might not qualify under the new one, and vice versa.

The exams themselves are 4.5-hour sessions on two consecutive days, three problems per day. Problems require full mathematical proofs. This is a hard pivot from multiple-choice AMC work — and why students who don't practice proof writing hit a wall here even with strong AMC scores.

Making USAMO or USAJMO means you are among the top few hundred math students in the country. Full stop. College admissions offices know what it means.

### MOP and IMO: The Top of the Mountain

MOP — the Mathematical Olympiad Program — is a roughly four-week summer camp held in June. Approximately 60–80 students are invited annually, selected from the top USAMO and USAJMO scorers plus additional female participants chosen for possible selection to the European Girls' Mathematical Olympiad (EGMO). According to MAA and AoPS sources, it has been held at Carnegie Mellon University and IMSA, among other sites. It is free.

From MOP, six students are selected to represent the US at the International Mathematical Olympiad (IMO), a two-day competition held in July, with three problems per day. In the 2025–2026 cycle, the IMO is scheduled for July in a different country each year.

Most families reading this book will never need to think about MOP. But it's useful to know it exists — because it's the destination that shapes the whole pipeline.

## Track 1.5: The Middle School Tier

Before high school, the competition math world has its own distinct ecosystem. Several competitions sit below the AMC 10/12 pipeline and are specifically designed for younger students.

### MATHCOUNTS: The Pipeline That Matters Most

MATHCOUNTS is the premier middle school math competition in the United States, open only to students in grades 6–8, with a maximum of three years of participation per student. It runs through four levels: School → Chapter → State → National.

The format has multiple rounds. The Sprint Round is 30 problems in 40 minutes, no calculator. The Target Round is eight problems in paired sets, calculator allowed. The Team Round is ten problems in 20 minutes for a team of four. At chapter, state, and national levels, there's a Countdown Round — head-to-head oral competition with 45 seconds per problem.

MATHCOUNTS matters for a few reasons. First, it builds exactly the problem-solving muscles you need for AMC 10 later — algebraic reasoning, number theory, clever counting, geometry. Second, it has a team component. A lot of kids who aren't drawn to individual competition find MATHCOUNTS genuinely fun because you're competing with and for your school. Third, the national-level competition is a serious achievement: the top four students from each state (224 competitors total) go to nationals, with travel covered.

One practical warning: according to MATHCOUNTS, participation is limited to three years total. Many families wait until 7th grade when they feel their child is "ready." That wastes 6th grade, which is meant for building experience. Start in 6th grade; peak performance in 7th and 8th grade is the normal arc.

### MOEMS: The Right First Competition for Most Students

MOEMS (Mathematical Olympiads for Elementary and Middle Schools) doesn't get talked about as much as MATHCOUNTS, but it may actually be the better entry point for most students.

MOEMS has two divisions: Division E (grades 4–6) and Division M (grades 6–8). Each division holds five monthly contests between November and March, each contest consisting of five problems in 30 minutes. A perfect season score is 25 points.

The format is forgiving in the best way. It's school-based and team-oriented. It doesn't require qualifying for anything. The problems are genuinely interesting but accessible — they introduce competition-style thinking without the pressure of a single high-stakes test. According to MOEMS, more than 120,000 students from every US state and 39 other countries participate annually.

If your 4th or 5th grader is mathematically curious and you want to introduce them to competitions, MOEMS Division E is where to start.

### ARML: The Team Event

ARML — the American Regions Mathematics League — is an annual national competition held simultaneously at five sites, on the first Saturday after Memorial Day. Teams consist of 15 members representing a geographic region. Students join via their state or regional math team's qualifying process.

The competition includes a Team Round (20 minutes, 10 problems), a Power Question (one-hour proof exploration), an Individual Round, and a Relay Round. It's particularly valuable for students who enjoy the team energy of competition and are at the AMC 10/12 preparation level or above. The proof component in the Power Question makes ARML unusual among timed competitions — it rewards a different kind of mathematical thinking.

### USAMTS: The Proof-Writing Alternative

USAMTS — the USA Mathematical Talent Search — is worth knowing about for students who find timed competition math unpleasant but love mathematical reasoning.

It's a free, proof-based competition administered by the Art of Problem Solving Foundation. Three rounds per year, five problems each, with over a month to solve them. You can use any resources except direct help from another person. Each problem is graded 0–5 with personalized written feedback.

According to AwesomeMath, a sufficiently high USAMTS score (approximately 68 out of 75) can qualify a student for AIME as an alternative to the AMC 10/12. That makes it both a training ground for proof writing and, for some students, their actual pipeline entry point.

### Math Kangaroo: The Low-Pressure Introduction

Math Kangaroo is an international competition held annually (March 2026 for the most recent cycle) for grades 1 through 12. It's low-stakes, accessible, and genuinely international in character. It's less a serious competition pathway and more a welcoming first competition experience. Multiple levels starting at grade 1.

## Track 2: Physics Competitions

The physics competition pipeline is simpler than math's: F=ma → USAPhO → US Physics Team Training Camp → IPhO. The pathway is administered by the American Association of Physics Teachers.

Most students begin this track in 10th or 11th grade — much later than math competitions, and that's by design. Physics competitions require actual physics knowledge, plus strong math skills. The 2026 F=ma exam was held in February. Target score to advance: about 15 out of 25.

The F=ma exam is 75 minutes, 25 multiple-choice questions, focused entirely on classical mechanics. It's the gateway. Approximately 400 students advance to USAPhO.

USAPhO is a 3-hour free-response exam — two 90-minute sections covering all introductory physics topics including mechanics, electromagnetism, thermodynamics, relativity, optics, and data analysis. This is where you need to actually write out your solutions. According to AAPT, medal distribution is roughly: Gold (top 10–12%), Silver (next 14–16%), Bronze (next 20–22%).

From USAPhO, approximately 20 students are invited to the US Physics Team Training Camp — a 10-day camp at the University of Maryland. The top 5 are selected to represent the US at the International Physics Olympiad.

The prerequisites matter: to do well at F=ma, you need strong algebra, trig, and at least one algebra-based physics course (AP Physics 1 level). To do well at USAPhO, you need calculus-based physics. The math competition background is genuinely useful here — USAPhO problems have a mathematical rigor that students with AIME-level preparation handle better than those without it.

One warm-up worth knowing: the Physics Bowl, also administered by AAPT, is a March competition with 40 multiple-choice questions. It's less selective and a good calibration before F=ma.

## Track 3: Science Competitions

The science track is different from both math and physics competitions in a fundamental way: the flagship competitions are not tests, they're projects.

### Science Olympiad: The Team Gateway

Science Olympiad is a team-based competition — 15 students per team — with 23 events spanning earth science, biology, chemistry, physics, and engineering. Division B covers grades 6–9; Division C covers grades 9–12. Tournaments proceed from invitational to regional to state to national.

Unlike ISEF, Science Olympiad doesn't require a student-driven research project. You study specific topics and compete in events, some of which involve building physical devices. It's more accessible and, for many students, more fun. It's also a good way to find out which science areas genuinely excite your child before committing to a year-long research project.

There is no individual entry pathway. You need a school team.

### ISEF and Regeneron STS: The Research Track

If your student wants to pursue serious science research and has a project they care about, the ISEF/STS track is the path.

Regeneron ISEF is the world's largest pre-college STEM competition — approximately 1,700 finalists from 60+ countries. But reaching it requires winning your regional affiliated fair first. The qualification pathway takes at minimum one year and more realistically two years: begin research, submit to a regional affiliated fair, win a slot, compete at ISEF International. This means research has to start in 9th or 10th grade at the latest. Students who start a project in 11th grade have only one shot.

Regeneron STS is different: it's application-based, restricted to 12th graders, and entirely US-based. According to the Society for Science, approximately 2,600 students entered the 2026 STS cycle — the largest pool since 1967. Of those, 300 are named Scholars and 40 become Finalists. STS has produced 13 Nobel Prize winners. Applications are due in November of senior year; research must be substantially complete before senior year begins.

Worth noting for math-leaning students: according to Rise Global Education, a math project won a top prize in Regeneron STS 2025. Theoretical and mathematical research is welcome, not just lab science.

### The Big Fork: Competition Math vs. Science Research (Around Grades 9–10)

By 9th or 10th grade, students who are strong in both math and science often face a real fork in the road. Competition math (AMC/AIME/USAMO) and science research (ISEF/STS) both demand serious time. Doing both well simultaneously is difficult.

The honest framework:

Competition math gives you direct intellectual challenge, immediate feedback, and a clearly defined pipeline. You know where you stand. Science research gives you a chance to make an actual contribution to human knowledge, and the narrative power in college applications that comes from original work.

Most families don't have to choose definitively until 10th grade. Science Olympiad lets students explore science broadly while staying compatible with competition math prep in 9th grade. By 10th grade, if research is calling, it's time to find a lab or mentor and begin a project — because the clock starts then, not in 11th grade.
