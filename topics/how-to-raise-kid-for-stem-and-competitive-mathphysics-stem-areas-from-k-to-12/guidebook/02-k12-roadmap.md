# Chapter 2: The K-12 Roadmap — What Happens When

---

Your eight-year-old just finished the AMC 8 for the first time. She's in second grade. The score was decent — not spectacular. Someone on a parent forum tells you that the serious competition students start MathCounts prep in sixth grade, that you should have been in AoPS by third grade, and that RSI applications are due during junior year. You close the tab more confused than when you opened it.

Here's the thing: you don't know if your kid took that test early, late, or exactly on time, because nobody gave you the map.

This chapter is the map.

It covers the full K-12 arc from kindergarten to senior year — what the right activities are at each stage, what the competition milestones are, what the key decision points look like, and when things actually have to happen versus when parents imagine they have to happen. Most of the urgency in the parent-forum ecosystem is manufactured. This chapter will help you tell the difference.

---

## The Full Arc in One Paragraph

The K-12 competitive STEM journey has four phases: exploration (K-2), foundation-building (grades 3-5), competition entry (grades 6-8), and specialization (grades 9-12). In the early years, the goal is building number sense and genuine love of puzzles — no competition prep, no pressure. In the middle years, the goal is developing the problem-solving instincts and algebraic foundation that competition math requires. By middle school, students who want to compete begin working through structured curriculum and entering their first real contests. High school is where the pathways diverge: some students pursue the math olympiad pipeline through AIME and USAMO; others pivot toward physics competitions; others move toward science research. The overwhelming majority of what looks like "falling behind" in the forum world is actually just students being exactly where they should be for their age.

---

## Grades K-2: Mathematical Thinking, Not Competition Prep

The goal in early elementary is simple and hard to rush: build number sense, arithmetic fluency, and a genuine love of mathematical puzzles. That's it.

There are no competitions your kindergartner needs to be in. There is no curriculum your first-grader is behind on. The parent urgency to start "real" enrichment at age five or six is understandable and almost always counterproductive. Children who are pushed into structured mathematical work before they're developmentally ready associate math with pressure, not pleasure. That association is hard to undo.

What actually works in K-2 is mathematical thinking embedded in daily life and play.

**Count and estimate constantly.** "How many steps to the car?" "If we need eight apples and we have three, how many more?" "About how many blueberries are in the bowl?" This builds number sense through real experience rather than worksheets.

**Play mathematical games.** Set, Blokus, Rush Hour, KenKen, Tangrams — these develop spatial reasoning, logic, and pattern recognition while feeling like play. A child who loves Set is building combinatorial thinking. A child who works through Rush Hour puzzles is developing logic skills. Neither of them is aware they're doing math.

**Read math-adjacent books.** *Bedtime Math*, *The Number Devil*, *Sir Cumference*, *Mathematicians Are People Too* — these normalize mathematical curiosity as entertainment rather than homework. A child who grows up in a house where math books are on the shelf alongside other books develops a different relationship to the subject.

**Let them be stuck.** This is the most important one. When your six-year-old encounters a puzzle she can't immediately solve, resist the urge to help. Productive struggle — sitting with a hard problem for ten or fifteen minutes without resolution — is the foundational skill that separates enrichment from drilling. You cannot shortcut this. You can only cultivate it by getting out of the way.

For gifted early-elementary students who are clearly hungry for more, Beast Academy Level 2 is accessible and genuinely excellent. It's a comic-book-style curriculum that gifted K-2 students tend to pick up and not put down. Many parents on gifted education forums describe children asking for "the next book" rather than needing to be told to do math. If that's your child, Beast Academy is the right move. If you have to coax your second-grader into it, wait six months and try again.

Math Kangaroo offers a competition for grades 1-2: 24 problems in 75 minutes, low-stakes, good fun. This is not competition prep. This is a puzzle test your kid might enjoy once a year. Treat it like that.

**The parent's job in K-2:** Provide access. Celebrate curiosity and persistence, not correct answers. Don't assign extra math homework on top of school math — that's how you create a child who hates math. Advanced work instead of routine work is the principle, not additional burden on top.

## Grades 3-5: Foundation Building

The goal shifts in third grade: develop the problem-solving instincts and algebraic thinking that competition math will eventually require. This is still not competition prep. It's foundation prep.

Beast Academy grades 3-5 is the core curriculum for this phase. It's specifically designed to develop the creative problem-solving approach that school math never teaches: understanding why things work, not just how to execute them. Students who complete Beast Academy through level 5 emerge with genuine mathematical maturity — not just fast computation, but reasoning skills.

Singapore Math is a strong alternative or complement, particularly for families who want strong place-value understanding and the model-drawing (bar model) approach that's particularly useful for multi-step word problems.

**First structured competitions:**

- **MOEMS Division E** (grades 4-6): Five monthly contests, 5 problems each, held at school. Low pressure, team-oriented, good first exposure to competition format.
- **Math Kangaroo** (grades 3-5): Annual contest, good benchmark, very non-threatening.

These competitions are valuable not because the results matter but because they give your child experience with the format: problems you haven't seen before, time pressure, the emotional experience of not knowing how to start. Building tolerance for that experience early, in low-stakes settings, is good preparation for when the stakes increase.

**The AoPS Prealgebra question:**

A common question in this grade band is when to start AoPS Prealgebra. The answer is: when the child has genuinely mastered Beast Academy Level 5 or equivalent. Grade level matters less than readiness. A student who's ready at fifth grade should start then. A student who's ready at fourth grade should start then. A student who isn't ready in fifth grade shouldn't be pushed.

**On Kumon and Mathnasium:**

Many parents ask whether these programs are worthwhile. The honest answer is that they serve a different function than competition math preparation. Kumon builds arithmetic automaticity through repeated drilling. That's a real and useful skill — there's value in not having to think about arithmetic so your mental energy can go to reasoning. But Kumon prepares students for fast, accurate computation, not for the creative problem-solving that competition math requires. Some families find Kumon useful in early elementary for building fluency, then transition to AoPS-style curriculum by third or fourth grade. Using both simultaneously often leads to the child experiencing math as a burden rather than a joy. Pick the goal and match the program to it.

**Key decision at the end of grade 5:**

Is your child ready for AoPS Prealgebra? This is the bridge to competition-level work. If yes, start it, even if that means summer between fifth and sixth grade. If not yet, stay with Beast Academy and revisit in sixth grade. Don't push the timeline; push the mastery.

## Grades 6-8: Competition Entry

This is where competition math begins in earnest, and where many families feel the urgency most acutely. Let's slow down and map it clearly.

The anchor competition for this phase is **MATHCOUNTS**: a structured competition series for grades 6-8 that runs school → chapter → state → national. It's the most important middle school math competition in the United States. School-level MATHCOUNTS is widely accessible; chapter and state levels are genuinely competitive; national MATHCOUNTS is among the most selective math competitions for this age group.

**AMC 8** is the other major competition: 25 questions, 40 minutes, for students grade 8 and below. Most competition-track students take it in grades 6-8. A score of 20 or higher on AMC 8 is a reasonable signal of readiness to begin AMC 10 preparation.

The standard preparation progression in this grade band:

| Resource | Type | When |
|----------|------|------|
| AoPS Prealgebra (if not complete) | Curriculum | Grade 6 start |
| AoPS Introduction to Algebra | Curriculum | Grade 6-7 |
| AoPS Introduction to Geometry | Curriculum | Grade 7-8 |
| AoPS Introduction to Counting & Probability | Curriculum | Grade 7-8 |
| AoPS Introduction to Number Theory | Curriculum | Grade 7-8 |
| MATHCOUNTS | Competition | Grades 6-8 |
| AMC 8 | Competition | Grades 6-8 |
| MOEMS Division M | Competition | Grades 6-8 |

Notice the Introduction series from AoPS covers four subjects: Algebra, Geometry, Counting and Probability, and Number Theory. Competition math integrates all of them. A student who only knows algebra can solve some AMC problems but will hit a ceiling quickly. The full Introduction series is the complete foundation for AMC 10/12 preparation.

**MathPath** (ages 11-14) deserves a mention here. It's a selective summer program specifically for younger students — unlike PROMYS and Mathcamp, which target high schoolers. Acceptance is competitive (roughly 30%), and alumni tend to describe it as a significant accelerant for students who are ready for peer community around serious math. It's worth applying for motivated students in this grade range.

**Key milestones in grades 6-8:**

- Completing AoPS Introduction to Algebra: you're ready to begin AMC 10 preparation.
- AMC 8 score of 20+: strong signal for AMC 10 work.
- MATHCOUNTS State qualifier: meaningful indicator of genuine competition talent.

**What the forums get wrong about this phase:**

The most common mistake parents make in this grade band is treating competition schedule like a checklist to complete as early as possible. Eighth graders who haven't completed the AoPS Introduction series take the AMC 10 and score poorly, then conclude they're not good enough. They're not bad at math. They're underprepared for the specific exam they just took. Readiness is skill-based, not age-based.

The other common mistake is over-scheduling. More programs do not accelerate progress. Two or three hours a week of focused, high-quality AoPS work will outperform six hours a week of scattered preparation every time. And a student who is burned out by eighth grade from too many programs and too many competitions is in a much worse position than one who arrives at ninth grade fresh and motivated.

**Competition math coaches typically advise:** Pick one curriculum (AoPS), one competition series (MATHCOUNTS for grades 6-7, AMC 8/10 for grades 7-8), and go deep. Don't diversify. Don't stack programs. Do the work and let the scores follow.

## Grades 9-12: Specialization

High school is where the pathway clarifies — and where the two major tracks diverge.

**The math competition pipeline:**

| Year | Competitions | What you're building |
|------|-------------|---------------------|
| 9th grade | AMC 10, USAMTS | AoPS Intermediate series; past AMC problems |
| 10th grade | AMC 10/12, AIME (if qualified) | AIME preparation; begin proof writing |
| 11th grade | AMC 12, AIME, USAMO (if qualified) | Olympiad-level books; summer programs |
| 12th grade | AMC 12, AIME, USAMO; possibly science research | Research and applications alongside competition |

The AMC 10 and AMC 12 are the gateway exams. AMC 10 is for students in 10th grade or below; AMC 12 is for all high schoolers. Scoring in roughly the top 2.5% of AMC 10 takers qualifies a student for the AIME — the American Invitational Mathematics Examination. AIME qualification is itself a meaningful achievement, and many students stop there. Students who score high enough on AIME qualify for the USAMO (or USAJMO, for younger qualifiers) — the American apex of the pipeline.

**USAMTS** deserves specific mention: it's a proof-based mail-in competition that gives students a month to solve five problems, with written feedback on their proofs. This is invaluable, because the jump from AIME (computation and cleverness) to USAMO (rigorous proof writing) is the hardest step in the pipeline, and most students have never written a mathematical proof before they hit it. Starting USAMTS in ninth grade builds those muscles before they're needed.

**Summer programs** become available and important in this phase:

- **PROMYS and Ross**: Selective number-theory intensive programs (roughly 10-30% acceptance) for rising 9th-12th graders. Deep mathematical experience, strong peer community.
- **Canada/USA Mathcamp**: Broader curriculum, equally selective. Often described by alumni as transformative for mathematical identity.
- **RSI (Research Science Institute)**: Entirely different in character — a research program for rising seniors, one of the most selective summer programs in the country at roughly 2-3% acceptance. Students work on original research with MIT faculty and graduate students. Applications in the fall of junior year for the summer before senior year.
- **MOP (Mathematical Olympiad Program)**: Invitation only, for top USAMO and USAJMO performers. Not something you apply to.

**The physics fork:**

Around ninth or tenth grade, students interested in physics competitions face a specific timing question. The main physics competition pipeline is F=ma → USAPhO (United States Physics Olympiad). F=ma is typically first taken in sophomore or junior year, after completing algebra-based mechanics. Students who qualify on F=ma advance to USAPhO semifinalist and finalist rounds.

The physics pipeline is shorter than the math pipeline — you need to take physics in school before competition preparation makes sense, which means serious physics competition preparation starts later. The good news is that the window is still adequate: a student who begins F=ma preparation in 10th grade and has two attempts before senior year can reach a meaningful level. Students should not feel they need to choose between math and physics in ninth grade — the timelines allow running both through most of high school, with physics ramping up in tenth and eleventh.

**The research track:**

Some students, around eighth or ninth grade, find that open-ended research is more interesting to them than competition performance. For these students, the path shifts toward science competitions: ISEF (International Science and Engineering Fair) and STS (formerly Siemens/Regeneron Science Talent Search). This requires beginning mentored research by 10th-11th grade. It's a different skill set from competition math — you're building scientific judgment and project management rather than speed on novel problems — and it suits a different kind of student.

**The grade 9 question you actually need to answer:**

Around ninth grade, a student and their family face one real decision: Is this student on a track toward USAMO-level competition? And are they interested in adding physics?

The reason this decision matters is not that the window closes — it doesn't, exactly — but that USAMO-track preparation in 10th and 11th grade is intensive enough that it's genuinely hard to add major new endeavors alongside it. Students who want to go deep on math competition should go deep. Students who want to add physics should add it deliberately, in a way that doesn't require sacrificing sleep or sanity.

Many students who imagined they would compete through senior year find, by 10th grade, that they've gotten what they wanted from competition math and want to shift toward research or other pursuits. That is a completely successful outcome. The pipeline is not a single path to a single destination. It's a collection of ramps that lead to different places, all of which are worth something.

## A Note on Timing: What's Early, What's Late, What's Fine

The urgency around competitive math timelines is dramatically overstated on parent forums.

**AMC 8 in second grade:** Very early. Not necessary. Not harmful if the child wants to do it. But not a sign of falling behind if they don't.

**Starting Beast Academy in third grade:** Normal. Right on time.

**Starting MATHCOUNTS in sixth grade:** Normal. The standard entry point.

**Taking AMC 10 for the first time in ninth grade:** Fine. Most students who go on to AIME took AMC 10 first in ninth or tenth grade.

**First F=ma attempt in junior year:** Completely normal. The pipeline accommodates this.

**Starting research in tenth or eleventh grade:** The standard timeline for ISEF and STS.

The only real "too late" moments in this roadmap are architectural: you can't do USAMO-level competition math without a foundation in the AoPS Introduction and Intermediate series, and you can't do physics competitions without having taken physics. Those are dependencies that require planning, not rushing.

Everything else is subject to your individual child's pace, readiness, and genuine interest.

**Chapter Takeaways:**

- The four phases are exploration (K-2), foundation (3-5), competition entry (6-8), and specialization (9-12). Each has a different primary goal.
- Foundation matters more than speed. A student who completes the AoPS Introduction series thoroughly in grades 6-8 is better prepared than one who rushes into AMC 10 without it.
- The two major tracks — math olympiad and physics competition — diverge around 9th-10th grade and can be run in parallel through most of high school.
- RSI is the one program with a hard timing constraint: applications are submitted in the fall of junior year for the summer before senior year.
- Most of the urgency you read about on forums is not real. The window is longer than it feels.
