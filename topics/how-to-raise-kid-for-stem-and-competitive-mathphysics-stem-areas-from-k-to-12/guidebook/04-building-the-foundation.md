# Chapter 4: Building the Foundation — The Elementary Years (K–5)

---

The instinct to start competition prep early is understandable. You've just read about AMC pipelines and MATHCOUNTS and all the rest, and your child is in second grade, and you're doing the math. If the national competitions are in middle school, and preparation for those takes years, and the best students started early, then shouldn't you be starting now?

The instinct is not wrong about the timeline. It's wrong about what "starting" means.

Elementary school is not the place for competition training. It's the place for something more important: building the kind of mathematical mind that makes competition training worthwhile when the time comes. Those are different activities. One produces a child who can perform in competitions under pressure. The other produces a child who actually loves mathematics. You want the second one. The second one also produces the first.

This chapter is about what that foundation actually looks like — year by year, with specific curricula and activities — and what it doesn't look like. The what-to-avoid section is not a formality. Missteps in elementary school are surprisingly hard to undo.

---

## The Foundation That Everything Else Rests On

At the heart of competitive mathematics — and, more broadly, at the heart of mathematical ability that persists and compounds through life — is a disposition, not a set of skills. The disposition is this: mathematical problems are interesting puzzles that reward careful thinking, and working on them is enjoyable.

That sounds almost too simple. But the research supports it. The Davidson Institute has found that mathematically gifted students who develop an identity as "math people" — who experience mathematics as something intrinsically enjoyable rather than a performance metric — maintain interest and engagement significantly longer than students who are primarily motivated by scores and external validation.

Experienced competition math coaches put it even more directly: the single most important thing you can do in elementary school is protect your child's curiosity about numbers. Everything else is teachable. Curiosity, once extinguished, is hard to relight.

This isn't abstract philosophy. It has concrete implications for what you do and don't do in these years.

## Grades K–2: Mathematical Play and Thinking Habits

Before grade 3, mathematical development is almost entirely about thinking habits, not content. The child who enters 3rd grade with genuine curiosity, comfort with uncertainty, and a playful relationship with numbers is better positioned than one who has been drilled on arithmetic facts and nothing else.

### What Mathematical Thinking Looks Like at This Age

Mathematical thinking at ages 5–8 is not arithmetic. It's pattern recognition. Spatial reasoning. The ability to hold multiple possibilities in mind simultaneously. Noticing that something doesn't follow a pattern and wondering why. Asking "what if" questions about numbers without being told to.

A child who looks at a row of blocks arranged 2, 4, 6, 8 and asks "what comes next?" — and then asks "what about 1, 3, 5?" — is doing mathematical thinking. A child who spots that 6 + 4 is the same as 7 + 3 and gets excited about it is doing mathematical thinking. These moments matter more than mastering multiplication facts a year ahead of schedule.

### Activities That Build Mathematical Thinking

**Puzzles.** LEGO sets, tangrams, Tetris (yes, Tetris), pattern blocks, and jigsaw puzzles all build the spatial reasoning that underlies geometry. They don't feel like math homework because they're not.

**Number games.** Games like Catan Junior, Blokus, Set, Yahtzee, and even Uno involve counting, probability, and strategy at an age-appropriate level. Playing these regularly and occasionally talking through your reasoning ("I took this road because it connects three cities") models mathematical thinking without naming it as such.

**Building and construction.** Anything involving measuring, counting materials, planning structures. The physical manipulation of quantities and shapes builds intuitions that formal education later puts language to.

**"What if" conversations.** "What if we had eight cookies to share among three people? Could we do it perfectly? What would we do?" This is not a test — it's an invitation. You don't need to guide the child to the right answer. The point is sitting with the problem together.

**Number books.** For young children, books like those in the Sir Cumference series use story and illustration to introduce mathematical ideas. The goal is familiarity and delight, not mastery.

### The "Why" Habit

The most important habit you can cultivate in these years is the habit of asking why. Not just "what's the answer?" but "why is that the answer?" Not just "how do you do this?" but "why does this method work?"

This is easy to model. When your child says "5 + 5 = 10," ask "why?" They'll probably look at you like you're confused. That's fine. Say "I mean, is there a reason it's 10 and not something else? Can you show me?" This teaches them that mathematical facts are not arbitrary — they follow from something. That's the beginning of mathematical proof.

### What NOT to Do

Here is where this chapter earns its keep.

**Don't drill arithmetic flashcards as the primary math activity.** Fluency with arithmetic facts is useful, and it will come. It comes from practice spread over time, not from intensive flashcard sessions. More importantly, if drilling flashcards is most of what your child associates with math, you're teaching them that math is rote and boring. You're not building a math person. You're building a child who can pass times-table quizzes and not much else.

**Don't rush to multiplication as a milestone.** Parents often treat multiplication as the badge of advanced elementary math. It isn't. A child who knows all their multiplication tables but can't reason about an unfamiliar pattern is not ahead. A child who doesn't know 7 × 8 instantly but can explain why multiplication is repeated addition and figure out any product from scratch is far better positioned.

**Don't introduce comparison anxiety.** "Your cousin learned fractions in 1st grade" is not mathematical enrichment. It's pressure. Comparison to peers at this age does one thing: it teaches the child that math is about being better than someone else, which is the opposite of the intrinsic motivation you're trying to build.

**Don't mistake speed for ability.** Some children think slowly and carefully and are exceptional mathematicians. Speed is a separate variable that can be trained later. If a competition eventually demands speed, you can add that. If you've killed a child's love of math by making them feel slow at age 7, you can't easily recover it.

## Grades 3–5: First Structured Enrichment

By 3rd grade, some students are clearly ready for more structure — not competition prep, but genuine enrichment that goes beyond what school provides. This is when curricula become relevant.

### Beast Academy: The Gold Standard for Gifted Elementary Students

Beast Academy is published by Art of Problem Solving. It covers grades 2–5 (published as Levels 2A through 5D) in a comic-book format built around a cast of monster characters who solve math problems together. It's available as physical books or an online subscription.

Beast Academy is genuinely hard. It's designed for gifted elementary students, and if your child isn't ready for it, that will be apparent quickly — the problems require multi-step reasoning even in the early levels. But for a mathematically curious child who has exhausted the challenge of normal school math, many parents on gifted education forums describe Beast Academy as generating spontaneous enthusiasm, with children asking for "the next book" rather than needing to be reminded to do their math.

The content is not drill. It's puzzles, logic, patterns, and mathematical ideas introduced through story. A Level 4 book might spend a chapter on the logic of combinations and permutations through a story about choosing toppings. A Level 5 book might introduce graph theory through a puzzle about bridges. The math is real and serious; the delivery is fun.

Beast Academy is not a replacement for everything else. It's enrichment — something to do because your child enjoys it, not a training regimen. If a child is doing Beast Academy resentfully because a parent insists, it's not serving its purpose.

### Singapore Math: A Different Philosophy, Equally Valuable

Singapore Math is built on a different approach: Concrete-Pictorial-Abstract (CPA). Students solve problems first physically (with blocks or manipulatives), then representationally (with diagrams), then abstractly (with symbols). The bar model method — representing relationships between quantities as lengths of bars — is particularly powerful for word problems.

Singapore Math builds number sense in a way that American school math curricula often don't. Students learn that the same quantity can be represented in multiple ways, and that choosing the right representation is itself a mathematical skill. This is directly useful later in competition math, where choosing the right frame for a problem is often the hardest step.

Singapore Math can be used alongside Beast Academy — they're not competing curricula. Beast Academy is more explicitly problem-solving focused; Singapore Math builds systematic fluency and reasoning.

### AoPS Prealgebra: The Bridge to Competition Math

The AoPS Prealgebra book is the bridge from elementary enrichment to competition math. It's typically appropriate for students finishing Beast Academy Level 5 — roughly equivalent to late 5th grade or early 6th grade for advanced students, but some students reach it later and that's fine.

The book covers everything from number theory basics and fractions through algebra, geometry, counting, and probability — all at a level of rigor and problem difficulty well above any school curriculum. It's the first AoPS text in the main sequence, and completing it is the genuine readiness signal for MATHCOUNTS and AMC 8 competition preparation.

Don't rush this. Starting AoPS Prealgebra before the student has solid arithmetic fluency and multi-step reasoning skills (which Beast Academy builds) leads to frustration and, often, abandonment of the AoPS ecosystem before it's had a chance to work.

### The Replacement vs. Add-On Question

At some point in grades 3–5, parents who are providing enrichment face a question: should this replace or supplement school math?

This is genuinely tricky, and the honest answer is that context matters. But many families who've navigated this path report that enrichment works best alongside normal school engagement, not as a wholesale replacement. School math provides social context, a baseline of content security, and important completion habits. A student who is "doing AoPS instead of school math" may ace the AoPS material while losing the habits of engagement that come from completing assignments alongside peers.

The exception: if your school is providing genuinely inadequate math instruction and your child is deeply disengaged from it, a formal homeschool arrangement using AoPS as the core curriculum can work well — especially given that AoPS Online School is accredited by ACS WASC and most courses are NCAA-approved. But this is a significant structural decision, not an informal add-on.

For most families: enrichment is an add-on. Find a few hours per week. Let the school curriculum run in parallel. Don't make competition math the primary identity of your child's mathematical education in elementary school.

## The School Math Gap: Why Good Students Struggle With Competition Problems

There is a predictable moment — it usually happens during a first AMC 8 or a first MATHCOUNTS sprint round — where a child who has been told their whole life that they're great at math encounters a problem they don't immediately know how to solve and feels genuinely confused. This is not a talent signal. It's a gap signal.

School math and competition math are different activities that share the same subject matter. The key differences:

**School math:** the teacher has just taught you a method; the homework is to apply that method correctly. Knowing which method to use is given to you.

**Competition math:** the problem is novel; no one has just told you the relevant technique; figuring out the approach is part of the problem. The student who solves a competition problem has not just calculated correctly — they've identified what kind of problem it is and chosen or invented an approach for it.

A child who gets perfect scores on school math tests may score below the median on AMC 8 on their first attempt. This is normal. It's a format-unfamiliarity signal, not a talent signal. Parents should say this explicitly to their child before any first competition. "The first time you take this, the problems will feel different from school math. That's expected. We're here to see what it's like, not to get a perfect score."

The bridge from school math to competition math is built through:

1. **Beast Academy and AoPS Prealgebra** — which introduce competition-style reasoning in a structured curriculum
2. **Exposure to problems without a given method** — just sitting with a problem that doesn't yield immediately is itself a skill to develop
3. **Multiple approaches to the same problem** — once a student solves something, ask "is there another way?" This is how mathematical creativity develops
4. **The AoPS Alcumus tool** — a free adaptive problem platform that adjusts difficulty based on performance, useful as a daily supplement

The gap doesn't close in one session or one competition. Expect two to three competition cycles before scores begin to reflect actual ability.

## First Competitions: When and Which Ones

### Math Kangaroo: The Zero-Pressure Introduction

Math Kangaroo holds competitions at multiple levels starting at grade 1. The format is accessible, the problems are often creative rather than purely computational, and the stakes are low. It's a good way to let a curious third or fourth grader experience what a competition feels like without any meaningful consequences. Don't overweight the score. Use it as a conversation-starter.

### MOEMS Division E: The Right First Competition for Most Students

MOEMS Division E (grades 4–6) is the best first formal competition for most mathematically engaged elementary students. Five monthly contests, five problems each, 30 minutes per contest, school-based. You don't qualify for it by doing something hard — you just sign up as a school team.

The problems are genuinely interesting and appropriately difficult. A 4th grader doing well in Beast Academy will find them challenging but solvable. A 5th grader finishing AoPS Prealgebra will find them manageable. The monthly cadence lets students build problem-solving confidence over time rather than treating a single test as a verdict.

The George Lenchner Medallion is awarded for perfect seasons (25/25). That's a real goal for an exceptionally strong student — and it's a specific, achievable target that gives a competitive child something to aim for.

### AMC 8: The Right Starting Point for Strong 5th and 6th Graders

The AMC 8 is appropriate as a starting competition for a student who has completed Beast Academy or is well into AoPS Prealgebra. For most students, this means 5th or 6th grade — not 3rd grade.

The 2025–2026 AMC 8 was held in a January window. 25 questions, 40 minutes, multiple choice. No penalty for wrong answers.

First-attempt score expectations: a student who has done Beast Academy through Level 5 might score in the 12–18 range. A student completing AoPS Prealgebra might score 18–22. A score of 20+ suggests readiness to begin AMC 10 preparation. These are directional benchmarks, not cutoffs — the point of taking AMC 8 in 5th grade is to learn what the competition feels like and identify gaps, not to qualify for anything.

### What "Good Scores" Look Like at This Age

Here's the calibration that parents need and rarely get:

A 5th grader scoring 15 on AMC 8 is doing well. A 6th grader scoring 18 is doing well. A student who scores 22+ as a 5th grader is exceptional by any standard. The child who scores 10 on their first try has identified areas to work on and has taken a first step. None of these scores are damning.

Competition math coaches consistently advise: do not praise scores; praise the quality of thinking and the willingness to engage with hard problems. A student who got a problem wrong but can explain why a specific approach seemed promising is developing exactly the right habits. A student who got the right answer by lucky guessing and can't explain why is not.

## The Starting-Too-Early Trap

Let's be direct about the failure mode that trips up the most well-intentioned families in this space.

The scenario: a parent reads about competition math, gets excited, and decides their 7-year-old who is good at arithmetic should start formal competition training. They buy AMC practice books. They sign up for the AMC 8 as a 2nd grader. They hire a tutor. The child, who has not yet developed the abstract reasoning and reading-comprehension-for-math skills that competition problems require, struggles and finds it unpleasant. The parent doubles down. The child begins to associate math with difficulty, pressure, and parental disappointment.

By the time the child is in 6th grade — the exact right time to engage meaningfully with MATHCOUNTS — they have a firmly negative relationship with anything called "competition math." They refuse. The parent can't understand why.

This is not hypothetical. It is a pattern that experienced competition math coaches describe regularly.

The problem isn't starting early. The problem is starting the wrong thing early. Starting Beast Academy in 2nd grade is genuinely good. Starting timed competition problem drills in 2nd grade is not. Starting AoPS Prealgebra in 4th grade when a student isn't ready is not. The activities have to match the developmental stage.

Mathematical curiosity is the thing you're protecting in elementary school. The competitions are the thing you're building toward. Don't let impatience cost you the former to speed up the latter.

## Grade-by-Grade Enrichment Map (K–5)

| Grade | Curriculum / Enrichment | Competitions | Readiness Signal for Next Step |
|-------|------------------------|--------------|-------------------------------|
| K–1 | Mathematical play: puzzles, games (Set, Blokus), pattern blocks, counting conversations | None — it's too early | Child asks "why?" questions about numbers spontaneously; enjoys numerical patterns |
| 2 | Beast Academy Level 2 (if ready); Singapore Math (parallel) | Math Kangaroo (optional, low-stakes) | Completes Beast Academy Level 2 problems independently; reads and understands multi-step problems |
| 3 | Beast Academy Levels 3A–3D; Singapore Math | Math Kangaroo | Solves Beast Academy Level 3 problems with minimal frustration; comfortable with multi-step reasoning |
| 4 | Beast Academy Levels 4A–4D; begin Singapore Math bar models systematically | MOEMS Division E (school team, recommended) | Finishes Beast Academy Level 4 without skipping; engages with MOEMS problems with genuine interest |
| 5 | Beast Academy Level 5 → AoPS Prealgebra (transition around mid-year or end of year) | MOEMS Division E; AMC 8 (for students finishing BA Level 5 or AoPS Prealgebra) | Completes Beast Academy Level 5; AoPS Prealgebra sections feel challenging but achievable; AMC 8 score 18+ |
| 5–6 transition | AoPS Prealgebra completion; begin AoPS Introduction to Algebra if ahead | AMC 8; MOEMS Division M (grade 6) | AMC 8 score 20+; interested in MATHCOUNTS prep; asks competition-style "why" questions independently |

**Key signals that a student is ready for the next level:**

- They seek out the next problem rather than being pushed toward it
- They can explain their reasoning, not just produce answers
- They experience frustration as informative rather than defeating — "I need to think about this differently" rather than "I can't do this"
- They remember interesting problems from previous sessions and return to them

**Key signals to slow down:**

- Visible dread before math enrichment sessions
- Only produces answers when given the method; cannot approach unfamiliar problem types
- Attributes success to luck or failure to stupidity rather than to approach and effort
- Needs significant external motivation (rewards, pressure) to engage

The goal of elementary school, on this path, is to produce a child who enters 6th grade with mathematical curiosity intact, some exposure to competition-style problems, basic algebraic reasoning, and a feeling that hard math problems are fun. That child is ready for MATHCOUNTS and AMC 8. That child has a real shot at the pipeline.

The goal is not to produce a child who has learned the most content the fastest. Content can always be added. Curiosity, once lost, is very hard to recover.
