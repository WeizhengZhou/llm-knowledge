# Chapter 13: Girls in Competitive Math and Physics

_You will understand the specific challenges girls face in competitive math and physics — and the concrete programs, strategies, and environments that actually help._

---

## The Funnel

Start with a number: 43%. That is roughly the share of AMC 12 takers who are female. Nearly half. If you looked only at the entry point to competitive math in America, you might think the gender gap was already closing.

Then look at the next level.

Of the students who qualified for the 2021 AIME — the exam that follows the AMC — approximately 21% were female. From 43% to 21% in a single step. At the USAMO level, female participation is around 10–14%. At the International Mathematical Olympiad, female gold medalists have accounted for 3.3% of all gold medals awarded since 1959.

This is the funnel. It narrows at each stage. And the pattern is not explained by ability.

Research by economists Glenn Ellison and Ashley Swanson at MIT found that girls at each performance level in the AMC pipeline are more likely to drop out of participating, and that high-achieving girls improve by less from year to year than boys with similar initial performance. The researchers were careful about this: the issue is not that girls who reach AIME are weaker than boys who reach AIME. It is that girls are more likely to stop competing before they reach AIME, and more likely to stop improving once they do. Something is happening that has nothing to do with mathematical talent.

That is what this chapter is about.

## What the Research Actually Says

The research on why girls leave competitive math pipelines has gotten sharper over the last decade, and some of the findings are both counterintuitive and actionable.

**Stereotype threat activates earlier than most parents expect.** Developmental psychology research has established that making gender identity salient before a math task disrupts girls' performance as early as ages 5–7 — kindergarten and first grade. This is not about telling a girl explicitly that boys are better at math. It is about the more diffuse signal of "you are being evaluated as a girl in a math context." That signal, which can come from environmental cues alone, consumes working memory and reduces performance on challenging tasks.

The mechanism works like this: when a person fears confirming a negative stereotype about their social group, the anticipatory anxiety itself occupies cognitive resources that would otherwise go into problem-solving. Stereotype threat is not a belief, it is a performance interference. A girl who consciously rejects the idea that boys are better at math can still be affected by stereotype threat if she is in an environment that makes gender salient before or during a difficult test.

**Belonging predicts who stays. Interest doesn't.** A 2022 study of 282 participants in the German Physics Olympiad found no significant gender differences in interest or subject value — girls in the physics olympiad found physics just as interesting and worthwhile as their male counterparts. What differed significantly was their sense of belonging (effect size d = 0.41) and their success expectations (d = 0.57). Girls felt physics was interesting. They did not feel physics was a place where they belonged.

The belonging finding matters because most parents and educators try to intervene at the interest level — more physics demos, more enthusiastic teachers, more STEM programming. Interest is not what's driving the gap. Girls who drop out of physics and math competitions are not bored. They feel like outsiders.

**The mother variable.** Research published in peer-reviewed psychology journals found that mothers' — not fathers' — implicit stereotypical beliefs about math ability moderate girls' vulnerability to stereotype threat. Girls whose mothers held stronger gender stereotypes about math showed measurable performance decrements under stereotype threat. Girls whose mothers strongly rejected those stereotypes did not show the same performance drop. This is not about mothers who explicitly tell their daughters "boys are better at math." It is about unconscious beliefs transmitted through micro-interactions — tone, expressed surprise at a daughter's achievement, subtle lowered expectations.

Fathers' beliefs did not show the same moderating effect. This is a finding worth sitting with.

**Identity bifurcation is the long game.** Around ages 12–14, many girls in competitive math environments experience what researchers describe as a conflict between "math person" identity and "girl" identity — not because they are being told these are incompatible, but because the social environment of competition math rarely models both simultaneously. The room is mostly boys. The celebrated achievers are mostly boys. The templates for what a "serious math competitor" looks like are mostly male. Girls who stay in the pipeline despite this often describe suppressing or compartmentalizing aspects of their identity. Girls who leave often describe the environment as "not for them" — which is a belonging statement, not a competence statement.

These findings are correlational. They identify mechanisms and patterns, not proven interventions. But they point clearly in a direction: the problem is environmental, not innate. And environments can be changed.

## The EGMO Pathway

In 2012, mathematicians created a competition with a specific premise: if the environment of elite competition math is producing an extreme gender gap at the top, build a different environment and see what happens.

The European Girls' Mathematical Olympiad — EGMO — is held annually, now with approximately 57 participating countries. The format is identical to the IMO: two papers on consecutive days, three problems per paper, 4.5 hours each, maximum 42 points total. The problems are at USAMO difficulty. This is not a junior version of the IMO. It is the IMO, for girls.

The US has dominated it.

Through 2025, the US leads the all-time EGMO medal table with 39 gold medals, 12 silver, and 5 bronze. In 2025, held in Prishtina, Kosovo, all four members of the US team won gold medals — they were the only team in the competition where every member earned gold. The team: Shruti Arun (Colorado, 17), Hannah Fox (California, 17), Alansha Jiang (Washington, 17), and Angela Liu (California, 17). They finished second overall. Two members ranked in the top 10 scorers globally.

The US does not send girls to EGMO through a separate application or a second-tier selection process. The pathway is the same AMC → AIME → USAMO/USAJMO pipeline that feeds the IMO team. The MAA explicitly routes top-scoring girl-identifying students into USAMO and USAJMO invitations each year, building the pipeline to MOP and then to EGMO Team Selection Tests. Girls do not apply to EGMO. They qualify.

This matters as a framing point. EGMO is not an accommodation. It is a different competition environment — one where the ratio of male to female competitors flips — and when you change that environment, American girls produced one of the most dominant competitive records in the history of the olympiad.

The funnel does not empty because the math is too hard.

## Programs Built for This

A realistic program landscape for parents of mathematically gifted girls:

**Math Prize for Girls** is the largest girls' math competition in the world. Hosted at MIT's Department of Mathematics each fall, it invites approximately 300 participants, with admission based on prior AMC 10 or AMC 12 scores. The prize pool is $100,000, with the top prize at $50,000. Eligibility runs through 11th grade. If your daughter is scoring well on the AMC and you are thinking about next steps, this is a natural target. The environment — several hundred girls competing at a high level at MIT — is simply different from anything in a standard co-ed competition. Many participants describe it as the first time they have felt like a majority in a serious math competition.

**AMC Young Women in Mathematics Prize** distributes $5,000 among the top five female-identifying scorers on each of the AMC 8, AMC 10, and AMC 12. Top section scorers receive certificates. This is the MAA's visibility mechanism within the existing pipeline — it doesn't change the competition, but it names high-achieving girls explicitly, which has its own signal value.

**Canada/USA Mathcamp** has made gender balance an explicit institutional goal, not an aspiration. The camp's student body is typically 45–50% girls and non-binary students. Mathcamp deliberately recruits female instructors and residential advisors, runs dedicated workshops on Women and Gender Minorities in Math, Impostor Syndrome, and implicit bias, and has built post-camp networks that reconnect alumnae at Math Prize for Girls and other events. This is not incidental — it required years of intentional work to achieve. For a girl who has competed in predominantly male environments throughout middle school, spending five weeks at a summer program where the gender ratio is flipped, and where female mathematicians are visible as instructors and role models, is a qualitatively different experience. Ask programs directly about their gender composition. Mathcamp will tell you plainly.

**MathILy**, an alternative to Mathcamp, is held at Bryn Mawr College — a historically women's college — which creates a different baseline environment.

**AWM (Association for Women in Mathematics)** runs several K-12 programs worth knowing. GirlsGetMath@ICERM is an intensive one-week day program for high school students hosted at Brown University's Institute for Computational and Experimental Research in Mathematics, focused on mathematics and computing. Sonia Kovalevsky Days are math workshops held at universities across the country, designed to bring together students and female mathematicians. The Julia Robinson Math Festival runs collaborative, non-competitive math events — useful particularly for younger students who need the math-is-enjoyable experience before the math-is-competitive experience.

**Epsilon Camp** serves highly gifted elementary students (ages 7–12) with a deliberately gender-balanced design. For parents of younger children, this is worth knowing about.

## The Role Models Who Changed What's Possible

Research is unambiguous that specific, named female role models are more effective than abstract statements about women's capability in mathematics. Not "women can do math too" — but specific people, specific achievements, specific paths from competition math to mathematical careers.

These are the ones worth knowing.

**Maryam Mirzakhani.** In 1994, as a teenager in Tehran, she won a gold medal at the IMO in Hong Kong — 41 out of 42 points. She was the first Iranian woman to win an IMO gold medal. The following year in Toronto, she returned and scored a perfect 42 out of 42. First Iranian, first person representing Iran, to achieve a perfect IMO score. She earned her PhD at Harvard in 2004, joined the Stanford faculty, and in 2014 became the first woman ever to win the Fields Medal — mathematics' highest honor, sometimes called the Nobel Prize of math, though it is awarded only to mathematicians under 40. The Fields Medal had existed since 1936. No woman had won it until Mirzakhani. She won it for work in the dynamics and geometry of Riemann surfaces — the same kind of deep mathematical thinking that competition math develops. She died of breast cancer in 2017 at the age of 40.

Tell your daughter this story. Not as an inspirational abstraction, but in the specifics: a girl who loved reading and never planned to be a mathematician, who competed in olympiads as a teenager, who became the best in the world. UNESCO designated May 12 — her birthday — as World Women in Mathematics Day.

**Melanie Matchett Wood.** In 1998, at 16, Wood became the first American woman ever to make the US IMO team. She earned a silver medal that year and again in 1999. At Duke University, she became the first American woman — and only the second woman in the history of the competition — to be named a Putnam Fellow, the top prize in American undergraduate mathematics. She completed her PhD at Princeton under Manjul Bhargava, himself an IMO gold medalist and later a Fields Medalist. She held positions at Stanford, Wisconsin, and Berkeley, and is now a professor of mathematics at Harvard. In 2022, she received a MacArthur Fellowship — the "genius grant." In 2025, she was elected to the National Academy of Sciences.

Wood's path is exactly the model that the competition math world needs: from IMO team to Putnam Fellow to doctoral mathematics to Harvard professor. The pipeline from competition math to serious mathematics research is real, and she walked it.

**Alison Miller** won a gold medal at the IMO in 2004 representing the United States, one of the few American women to reach that level. She went on to study mathematics at Harvard and completed a PhD at Princeton.

**Sherry Gong** represented the US at the IMO in 2005 and 2007, winning gold in 2007. At Harvard, she scored over 100 points in Math 55, the university's legendary most difficult undergraduate course. She completed her PhD at MIT.

And then there is the 2025 US EGMO team — Shruti Arun, Hannah Fox, Alansha Jiang, Angela Liu — four seventeen-year-olds who swept every gold medal in their competition. This is not history. This is happening now.

## Physics: The Harder Problem

The situation in physics competitions is more difficult than in math, and it is worth being honest about that rather than pretending the two fields are equivalent.

At the US Physics Olympiad training camp, documented instances show female participation around 5% — far below even the already-low figures in math olympiad programs. In international physics olympiad programs across multiple countries, female participation rates range from zero to around 14%. A 2019 systematic review in the Journal of Research in Science Teaching documented female underrepresentation in physics competitions across multiple countries and cultures, consistently more severe than in mathematics.

The German Physics Olympiad research (2022, 282 participants) that we discussed earlier focused specifically on physics, and the findings are pointed. Physics carries a stronger cultural "male domain" association than even mathematics. When female competitors endorse the negative stereotype about female talent for physics, their sense of belonging drops dramatically (correlation r = -0.39). Male competitors show essentially no relationship between holding that stereotype and their sense of belonging (r = -0.01). The stereotype doesn't affect the majority. It affects the girls who are already in the room.

There is no EGMO equivalent for physics. No US girls-specific physics competition, no girls-only physics olympiad pathway, no Math Prize for Girls analog. This is an acknowledged gap in the physics community, and it is real. Math has spent the last 15 years building a parallel infrastructure for girls. Physics has not done the same work.

What this means practically, if you have a daughter who is interested in physics competitions: the social environment of preparation matters more than it does for math, because the structural supports are thinner. For physics, specifically, seek programs that have explicitly worked on gender inclusion. The belonging problem is the proximate cause of dropout — not the physics itself.

The F=ma exam and the USAPhO pathway are exactly as achievable for a girl as for a boy. The math is the same. The physics is the same. What differs is the environment she will be preparing in and competing in, and the absence of a girls-specific competition to provide an alternative experience. Keep that distinction clear.

## What You Can Actually Do

Seven concrete things, based on what the research shows actually moves the needle.

**1. Start before the stereotype activates.**
The data shows stereotype threat effects as early as ages 5–7. Girls who build a strong math identity in elementary school — before the social environment of competition math becomes predominantly male, before the middle school identity pressures kick in — carry that identity forward more durably. Early math circles, early AMC 8 preparation, and Epsilon Camp for the youngest students all matter precisely because they build foundation before the friction starts.

**2. Examine your own beliefs — particularly if you are a mother.**
The research on the mother variable is consistent and worth taking seriously. If you believe, even quietly, that boys are more naturally mathematical than girls, your daughter will sense it. Not through explicit statements, but through the micro-interactions that accumulate over years: the tone when she struggles, the level of expressed surprise when she succeeds, the ambition of the goals you set for her. This is not blame — these beliefs are culturally transmitted and often unconscious. It is worth examining them directly.

**3. Choose environments deliberately.**
Not all summer programs are equivalent for a girl in competitive math. Mathcamp's 45–50% gender balance didn't happen by accident — it required institutional commitment sustained over years. When evaluating summer programs, ask directly: what is your current gender breakdown? What do you do to create belonging for girls and non-binary students? Programs that have done this work will be able to tell you specifically. Programs that haven't will give vague answers.

**4. Name the role models explicitly and specifically.**
Generic "women can do math" messaging does not move the needle. Named, specific people with specific achievements do. Tell the Mirzakhani story in detail: the girl who wanted to be a writer, the perfect IMO score, the Fields Medal, the geometry of Riemann surfaces. Tell the Wood story: the first American woman on the IMO team, the Putnam Fellowship, Harvard professor. Point to the 2025 EGMO team and describe what they actually did — four seventeen-year-olds who swept the gold in a competition at USAMO difficulty. Make the role models concrete.

**5. Target Math Prize for Girls.**
If your daughter is scoring well on the AMC, Math Prize for Girls is the logical next goal. The application process requires prior AMC 10 or AMC 12 scores; the deadline is May 31. The experience of competing among several hundred girls at MIT is qualitatively different from any co-ed competition she has entered, and the community of Math Prize alumnae — including those who reconnect at Mathcamp — is a lasting network.

**6. Don't narrate the gender gap in ways that frame it as natural.**
Parents in the competition math community sometimes unintentionally signal to their daughters that the male dominance of the field is innate rather than constructed. How you talk about the statistics matters. "Most of the kids at competitions are boys" is a description. "Boys tend to be more naturally competitive" is a belief that transmits itself. Describe the people who are breaking the pattern. Describe the EGMO. Describe Melanie Wood as a sixteen-year-old making the IMO team when no American woman had done it before.

**7. For physics: be deliberate about the preparation environment.**
Since no EGMO-equivalent exists for physics, a girl preparing for the USAPhO pathway will need to find belonging some other way. Mixed-gender study groups, programs with explicit gender inclusion work, and proximity to female physics mentors and role models matter more here than in math, where the structural supports are better developed. The APS Committee on the Status of Women in Physics and the Society of Physics Students both run programs, though none are specifically competition-focused. Be intentional about building a preparation community that doesn't inadvertently reinforce the "physics is for boys" signal.

The competitive math and physics world is not neutral. It has a history, a demographic pattern, and an environment that developed without girls specifically in mind. That is changing — the EGMO is changing it, Mathcamp is changing it, Math Prize for Girls is changing it, and the careers of Mirzakhani and Wood and the 2025 EGMO team are changing it. Your job is to know where the friction is and route around it where you can.

## A Note on What This Chapter Is Not Saying

This chapter has covered real challenges. But it is worth being explicit about what the research does not say.

The funnel is not fate. Girls who reach the AIME perform comparably to boys at that level. Girls who reach the USAMO can win gold at EGMO. Girls who compete in physics olympiads show the same interest and subject knowledge as boys at equivalent levels. The ability is there. The funnel is not a natural filtering of talent. It is a product of specific, identifiable mechanisms — stereotype threat, belonging deficits, environmental design — that can be partially addressed with specific, identifiable actions.

Your daughter does not need special accommodation or lowered standards. She needs accurate information, a well-chosen environment, and the stories of the women who went all the way.

### The Physics Gap in Brief

Physics competitions have a more severe gender gap than math (USAPhO training camp documented at 5% female vs. math's 10–14% at USAMO level). No girls-specific physics competition exists in the US. The proximate cause of dropout is belonging, not interest or ability. For physics specifically: environment of preparation matters more; seek out mixed-gender study groups and programs with explicit inclusion work. The physics is the same — the environment is not.
