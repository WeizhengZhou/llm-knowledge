# College Squash Recruiting

## Overview

This guide covers which colleges recruit for squash, what ranking level is competitive for recruitment, the recruiting timeline, and the realistic admissions advantage of squash. It is written for Bay Area parents considering squash as part of a college strategy.

## How Many Colleges Have Varsity Squash?

The number of colleges with varsity squash programs varies by source and changes year to year. For the 2025-2026 season, CSA lists 34 men's and 31 women's varsity programs. Some sources cite 37 total programs, which may reflect a different count methodology or an earlier season.

For the 2025-2026 season, the College Squash Association lists 34 men's and 31 women's varsity programs. These are concentrated at Ivy League and top D3 schools, with no athletic scholarships available. College squash teams are small (typically 10-20 players), resulting in limited annual recruiting spots.

Stanford University has a women's varsity squash program, which is the only West Coast varsity squash program. There is no men's varsity squash program on the West Coast. Bay Area juniors targeting college squash are overwhelmingly aiming at East Coast schools.

## The Talent Pool

According to xfactoradmissions.com, the US Squash nationally ranked junior pool consists of approximately 142 BU19 and 104 GU19 players. This small pool is the primary reason squash offers a meaningful recruiting advantage: there are far fewer competitors for college roster spots compared to mainstream sports.

According to Sparkadmissions, college squash varsity teams typically consist of 10-20 players, and coaches may recruit only one or two players per year.

## What Ranking Is Needed?

According to xfactoradmissions.com, rough estimates for college squash recruiting competitiveness are: top 10-20 nationally for Ivy League programs (Harvard, Yale, Princeton), top 20-50 for strong D1 programs (UPenn, Columbia), and top 50-100 for elite D3 programs (Amherst, Williams, Tufts). These are approximate figures that vary by program, year, and gender.

## Is Squash an Admissions Advantage?

According to xfactoradmissions.com (citing OpenAdmits/Harvard data), recruited athletes at Harvard are approximately 20 times more likely to be accepted, with athletes rated 1-2 on the admissions scale having an 83% acceptance rate and athletes rated 4 having a 70.5% rate versus 7.6% for non-athletes. Note: these figures are Harvard-specific and should not be generalized to all Ivy League schools.

Ivy League and Division III schools do not offer athletic scholarships for squash. The advantage is in admissions probability, not financial aid.

## Academic Requirements

Recruits must meet the same academic standards as other admits. Strong GPA (3.7+ for Ivy/elite D3 targeting), strong standardized test scores, and rigorous coursework (AP/honors) are expected. Apply via Early Decision to demonstrate interest.

## Geographic and Socioeconomic Reality

According to xfactoradmissions.com, squash is 'heavily east coast centric' with a minor concentration in California; sources for college squash teams come from 'a relatively small clutch of elite private high schools in the northeast, and a couple in California.'

According to xfactoradmissions.com, nearly 90% of Ivy League squash players attended private high schools with fees around $30,000.

## Bay Area Context

- Stanford women's varsity is the only West Coast program
- According to Stanford Recreation, non-Stanford-affiliated families cannot access Stanford squash courts as a training base; the nearest accessible alternative is Squash Zone in Redwood City (approximately 3 miles from Stanford campus)
- JCT #2 in Fremont brings college coaches to the Bay Area annually
- Bay Area juniors compete against a deep Northeast talent pool but benefit from a small nationally ranked field

## Recruiting Timeline

| Stage | Typical Timing |
|-------|---------------|
| Build national ranking | Ages 12-15 (U13-U15 age groups) |
| Attend JCT events (visibility) | Ages 13-17 |
| Initial coach outreach | Summer before junior year (age 16) |
| Official campus visits | Junior year fall/winter |
| Early Decision application | Senior year November |
| Commitment | Senior year December-March |

## Common Mistakes

- **Starting the college conversation too late.** Building a competitive national ranking takes 3-5 years of focused tournament play. Families should understand the timeline by age 11-12.
- **Assuming squash alone gets you in.** Recruits must meet academic standards independently; squash provides an admissions boost, not a bypass.
- **Ignoring the geographic disadvantage.** Bay Area players have fewer local training partners and tournaments than Northeast players. Budget for East Coast travel.
- **Overestimating the number of spots.** With teams of 10-20 players recruiting 1-2 per year across 30+ programs, total national spots per gender may be approximately 60 per year.
