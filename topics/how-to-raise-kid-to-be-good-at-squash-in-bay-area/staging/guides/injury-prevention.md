# Injury Prevention and Safety

## Overview

This guide covers the primary injury risks in junior squash, mandatory safety equipment, and prevention strategies. Eye injuries are the most serious acute risk, but are almost entirely preventable with proper eyeguards.

## Eye Protection (Non-Negotiable)

Squash eyeguards must meet or exceed the ASTM-F3164 standard; US Squash mandates eyeguards for all juniors in all sanctioned events. Basic certified eyeguards cost approximately $20-40.

According to a synthesized safety source, the World Squash Federation has recommended mandatory eyewear for all juniors since 1996.

According to ROSPA (UK Royal Society for the Prevention of Accidents), a squash ball traveling at 150 KPH has energy impact comparable to a .22 bullet; approximately 2,000 eye injuries per year occur from squash balls and 2,400 from racquets.

These injuries are classified as "almost totally preventable with appropriate protective equipment." Regular eyeglasses and open eyeguards (no lenses) are not safe substitutes.

## Other Common Injuries

### Lower Body
- **Ankle sprains:** Most common squash injury, caused by sudden direction changes. Prevention: squash-specific court shoes, proper warm-up
- **Knee injuries:** Patellofemoral syndrome from repetitive court movement. Prevention: gradual training load increases, strength work
- **Achilles tendon strain:** From explosive pushing-off movements. Prevention: proper footwear, warm-up

### Upper Body
- **Wrist strain:** From ball impact, especially backhand shots. Prevention: proper technique under coach supervision, avoid gripping the racket too tightly
- **Shoulder overuse:** In higher-volume training programs. Prevention: balanced training loads, rest days

### Overuse (Special Concern for Adolescents)
- Stress fractures in growing bones are a high concern in adolescent athletes
- Prevention: rest days, gradual load increases, cross-training

## Court Collision Risks

Squash courts are small with two players moving at speed. The let/stroke system is designed to manage collision risk. Juniors learn these rules as part of the referee exam requirement (see [[tournament-rankings]]).

## Prevention Checklist for Parents

1. Ensure certified ASTM-F3164 eyeguards are worn at ALL times on court
2. Invest in squash-specific court shoes (not running shoes)
3. Warm up properly before each session
4. Work with a certified coach to establish correct technique early
5. Build training load gradually; no sudden volume spikes
6. Ensure adequate rest days between sessions
7. Cross-train rather than squash-only for developing players
8. Monitor for overtraining symptoms (chronic pain, fatigue, personality changes)

## Cross-Training for Injury Prevention

According to Onyx Squash Academy (citing sports science research), multi-sport participation until puberty reduces injury risk, prevents burnout, and enhances long-term athletic development compared to early specialization; specializing before age 12 is associated with higher dropout rates.

According to Onyx Squash Academy, approximately 70% of squash conditioning work should take place on-court to maintain a squash-specific fitness foundation. The remaining 30% should include complementary activities: swimming (low-impact cardio), agility drills, plyometrics, and running.

According to Onyx Squash Academy, structured strength training is appropriate from approximately age 12-13+ with proper form guidance; children under 12 should focus on movement patterns and fun over structured strength training.

## Overtraining

According to Onyx Squash Academy, if burnout occurs, athletes typically need 4 to 12 weeks of rest before gradually returning to sports. See [[development-pathway]] for details on recognizing burnout vs. normal frustration.

## Common Mistakes

- **Allowing a child to wear regular eyeglasses on court instead of certified squash eyeguards.** Regular eyeglasses and open eyeguards (no lenses) are not safe substitutes for ASTM-F3164 certified squash eyeguards. This is the single most dangerous equipment mistake in junior squash. Certified eyeguards cost $20-40 and are non-negotiable.
- **Using running shoes instead of squash-specific court shoes.** Running shoes lack lateral support for the side-to-side movement pattern in squash and significantly increase ankle sprain risk (the most common squash injury). Squash-specific or indoor court shoes with non-marking soles and lateral reinforcement are required at all facilities.
- **Skipping warm-up because the session is short.** Junior clinics at [[squash-zone]] are 45-75 minutes. Parents and coaches may be tempted to skip warm-up to maximize court time. Ankle sprains and Achilles strains most commonly occur in the first 10 minutes of play without adequate warm-up. Even a 5-minute dynamic warm-up (lunges, high knees, lateral shuffles) reduces injury risk substantially.
- **Ignoring overuse symptoms in adolescents.** Stress fractures in growing bones are a particular concern in adolescent squash players. Chronic pain that persists between sessions, limping after play, or declining performance are warning signs — not normal soreness. A rest period is far less costly than a stress fracture that sidelines a player for months.
